# The Price of Unspoken Oaths

*A House of the Dragon One-Shot — Sequel to "The Weight of Unasked Favors"*

---

## I. Blood on the Red Stone

The birthing chamber smelled of iron, wet linen, and the same crushed myrrh that had choked Maegor's Holdfast fourteen years ago, though the woman screaming upon the pillows refused to die for any man's knife.

"Push, Princess," the midwife urged between Princess Rhaenyra's knees, her sleeves rolled above her red elbows. "The head is crowning—one more breath—"

At the head of the bed, Lady Shaera Targaryen braced her hip against the carved oak post and held Rhaenyra's left hand in both of hers. Rhaenyra's knuckles were white as boiled bone, her rings biting deep into Shaera's palms as she arched her back off the sweat-soaked mattress with a guttural, feral roar that had nothing of courtly music in it and everything of Syrax shrieking from the Dragonpit below the hill.

Fourteen years had passed since Queen Aemma Arryn had bled her life out upon these same floorboards. Rhaenyra was nine-and-twenty now, her silver-gold hair plastered across her brow, while Shaera stood beside her with the sleeves of her crimson gown pinned back from the blood.

With a wet, rushing slide and a sudden slap of the midwife's palm, a thin wail split the steam of the chamber.

Rhaenyra collapsed back against the bolsters, her chest heaving, a fierce, triumphant laugh breaking through her teeth as the midwife lifted the squirming, blood-streaked boy into the light of the braziers.

"A son, Princess," the woman said. "Hale and loud."

Shaera let out the breath she had been holding since dawn and reached for a bowl of warm rosewater to wipe the sweat from her cousin's temples. When Rhaenyra took the swaddled infant and brushed back the wet cap of dark brown hair—as brown as Jacaerys's and Lucerys's, and as brown as Ser Harwin Strong's curls—neither cousin spoke the thought aloud.

Before the midwife had even tied the cord of the afterbirth, the oak door opened and the serving girl Talya stepped inside, her eyes fixed carefully on the rushes.

"The Queen has sent word, Princess," the girl said. "Her Grace wishes to see the babe. Immediately."

The warmth drained out of Rhaenyra's face, leaving behind cold Valyrian stone. "The placenta has not even cooled."

"She was most insistent, Your Grace."

Rhaenyra's jaw set. Pushing back the bloodied linen sheet, she swung her bare, trembling legs over the side of the bed.

"Rhaenyra, no," Shaera said sharply, catching her cousin's elbow as Rhaenyra swayed on her feet. "Let Laenor carry him up once he comes from the yard, or let me take him. Your stitches have not even been set."

"If I send my son into that woman's solar without me," Rhaenyra hissed through clenched teeth, bracing one hand on Shaera's shoulder as the maids hurried forward with a robe of red-and-black brocade, "she will have the whole court whispering before the bells ring noon. Help me with the laces."

There had never been any turning Rhaenyra when her blood was up, any more than there had been turning Shaera's father. When Ser Laenor Velaryon arrived breathless from the tiltyard a moment later—all bright smiles and wine-sweetened warmth until he saw the set of his wife's mouth—it was Shaera who walked half a pace behind them out into the corridor, her hand hovering ready beneath Rhaenyra's left elbow while Laenor supported her right.

It was a march through purgatory.

Up the winding stone stairs of the holdfast they went, past whispering washerwomen and wide-eyed pages, past stewards who bowed and then stared down at the dark crimson drops trailing across the flagstones behind the Princess's hem. With every step Rhaenyra's breath came in ragged hitches, her fingernails digging through the wool of Shaera's sleeve.

At the top of the gallery outside the Queen's apartments stood Ser Criston Cole in the gleaming white plate and snowy cloak of the Kingsguard. Fourteen years had hollowed the Dornish-marches boy who had once caught Rhaenyra's favor on his lance into a hard, bitter sentinel; his dark eyes flicked coldly over Rhaenyra's sweat-matted hair and over Shaera's blood-spotted cuff before he knocked once and swung the doors wide.

Queen Alicent's solar was bright, airy, and smelled of lemon-oil and Seven-Pointed incense.

Alicent rose from her cushioned chair in a gown of deep, mossy Hightower green, her auburn hair bound up beneath a golden lattice. Gone was the anxious fifteen-year-old girl who had waited at the foot of the Dragonpit carriage tearing her cuticles raw; in her place stood a Queen who wore her piety like mail.

"Rhaenyra," Alicent said, her brows lifting in a show of concern that never reached her eyes. "You should be resting in your bed. Ser Laenor could have carried the child."

"I thought I might come myself," Rhaenyra said, sinking onto the velvet settee with a low, involuntary grunt of pain as the afterbirth gave way beneath her skirts.

And then Shaera's heart stopped in her throat.

For Alicent was not alone in the solar.

Standing two paces behind the Queen's chair, by the tall mullioned window overlooking Blackwater Bay, stood a tall knight in a travel-stained surcoat of smoke-grey wool clasped at the throat with a silver beacon tower.

Fourteen years had broadened Ser Gwayne Hightower's shoulders and threaded the first faint silver hairs at his chestnut temples. A clean, pale scar—the mark of Prince Daemon's tourney tilt—cut diagonally through his left eyebrow and down across the ridge of his cheekbone, giving his fair southern face a weathered, hawkish edge. He held a rolled leather dispatch case from Oldtown in his gloved left hand.

When Shaera stepped into the room behind Rhaenyra, Gwayne's grey-green eyes met hers across twenty feet of polished Myron carpet.

Two years had passed since they had last met in a fisherman's chapel near Duskendale after Lady Rhea Royce died upon the hunting tracks of Runestone—ten stolen minutes while Shaera's eyes were still dry and terrible with the knowledge that her father had been in the Vale that week.

Now, standing behind his sister's chair while Rhaenyra bled into the Queen's velvet cushions, Gwayne inclined his head in a bow of irreproachable, distant courtesy.

"Princess Rhaenyra," Gwayne said evenly. "Lady Shaera. Permit me to offer Oldtown's felicitations upon the birth of your son."

"Ser Gwayne," Rhaenyra panted, forcing a brittle smile as she handed the bundle into Alicent's waiting arms. "We did not know the winds had blown another Hightower up the Roseroad."

"I rode in with the morning tide, Princess," Gwayne replied smoothly. "My uncle Lord Hobert sent the autumn tallies for His Grace's council."

Shaera kept her hands folded over the bloodstain on her sleeve, her violet eyes cool as glass. "The roads must be dry in the Reach, Ser Gwayne, if you made the River Gate before noon."

"Dry enough, Lady Shaera," he said, and only she could hear the faint, familiar catch beneath the formal cadence of his voice. "Though the dust has a way of clinging to a man's throat."

By the hearth, Queen Alicent parted the linen swaddling around the infant's head, her gaze fixing for three long, silent seconds upon the tuft of dark brown hair before she looked over her shoulder at Laenor.

"Do keep trying, Ser Laenor," Alicent said, her voice dripping with honeyed venom. "Soon or late, you may get one who looks like you."

Shaera felt Rhaenyra stiffen beside her on the settee. Before either woman could draw blood with the next word, the side doors burst open and King Viserys hobbled into the solar, leaning heavily on his cane.

Time and the Iron Throne had eaten the King alive. His left arm was wrapped in linen bindings beneath a loose velvet sleeve, his cheeks sunken and spotted with grey sores, his hair thin as cobwebs; yet at the sight of the bundle in his wife's arms, his ruined face lit with the old, desperate warmth.

"Let me see him!" Viserys wheezed, shuffling forward to take the child into his one good arm. "Ah, look at that nose—a true fighter's nose! And what is his name to be?"

"Joffrey, Your Grace," Laenor said quickly.

"Joffrey," the King repeated, chuckling fondly as he glanced up at Shaera standing sentinel behind his daughter's shoulder. "You see, Shaera? Another cousin for the nursery! Soon the Dragonpit will scarcely hold the cradles, let alone the beasts."

As the King laughed and Ser Harwin Strong slipped through the doorway to gaze upon the newborn with naked, foolish pride in his dark eyes, Shaera risked one glance back toward the window.

Gwayne was not looking at the babe, nor at Harwin Strong. He was looking down at the hem of Rhaenyra's robe, where a fresh dark stain was spreading across the Queen's pale carpet, and then up at Shaera—his scarred brow drawn tight with the unspoken warning they had shared for half their lives: *The floor is giving way beneath us.*

## II. Beneath the Dome and the Yard

In the cavernous subterranean vaults of the Dragonpit, the heat smelled of sulfur, charred mutton bones, and eighty years of banked fire.

Late that afternoon, while Rhaenyra slept under the maester's poppy-milk and the boys took their lessons with the Dragonkeepers in the upper amphitheater, Shaera walked alone down the torchlit basalt ramp to the deep eastern cavern where Silverwing laired.

Her dragon had grown vast in the fourteen years since the Heir's Tournament. Coiled like a mountain of hammered pewter upon the heated stone floor, Silverwing lifted her horned head as Shaera stepped past the iron chain, her amber eyes slitting with a low, rumbling purr that vibrated through the soles of Shaera's riding boots.

*"Kessa, ñuha zaldrīzes,"* Shaera murmured, laying her ungloved palm flat against the warm, scarred scales along Silverwing's snout. *Easy, my dragon.*

With her mother dead in the Vale and Ser Gerold Royce still disputing her claim to Runestone's bronze gates, Silverwing was the only house Shaera truly owned. Prince Daemon had lived across the Narrow Sea in Pentos for ten years with his wife Lady Laena Velaryon and their daughters Baela and Rhaena, sending Shaera brief letters twice a year that never once mentioned Runestone.

As Shaera climbed back up the winding basalt steps toward the upper gallery, a sudden roar of dragonflame echoed from the lower tunnels, followed by the scrambling clatter of boots.

Around the bend stumbled young Prince Aemond Targaryen.

The Queen's second son was ten years old, lanky and pale, his dark green tunic streaked with soot and grease, his hands trembling violently at his sides. An hour earlier, Aegon, Jace, and Luke had mocked him in the upper yard with a pig fitted with wooden wings—a cruel jest that had driven Aemond down into the deep pits alone.

Shaera caught the boy by the shoulder before he could pitch headlong down the steps. "Aemond! Seven hells, your hair is singed—which vault did you go into?"

Aemond jerked his shoulder out of her grip, his jaw clenching with all the brittle, defensive pride of a dragonless Targaryen surrounded by riders. "Leave me be, Cousin," he spat, though his voice cracked with terror. "I do not need your pity."

He bolted up the stairs toward the daylight before she could call him back.

By the time Shaera returned to the Red Keep an hour later, the sun was slanting gold across the high curtain wall, and the whole castle seemed to have gathered around the lower training yard.

From the stone balcony above the smithy, King Viserys and his Hand, Lord Lyonel Strong, leaned over the balustrade to watch the boys at arms. Below on the packed dirt, Ser Criston Cole paced like a white wolf among the four princes—Aegon and Aemond in Hightower green-and-black on one side, Jacaerys and Lucerys in Targaryen red on the other—while Ser Harwin Strong leaned against the timber weapon racks with his arms folded across his broad chest.

Shaera stepped into the shaded stone arcade flanking the yard, where the smell of oiled mail and wet sand hung cool out of the sun—and stopped three paces from the archway.

Ser Gwayne Hightower stood just inside the shadow of the colonnade, his hands resting lightly on the pommel of his sheathed longsword as he watched his nephews in the dirt.

Neither of them looked at the other. They stood four feet apart, both facing the sunlit yard as if they were two strangers sharing a patch of shade.

"You did not write that you were coming," Shaera murmured, her lips scarcely moving as Aegon knocked young Jace's wooden sword aside to a bark of instruction from Ser Criston.

"I did not know until four days before I rode," Gwayne answered in the same low, level tone, his eyes fixed on the yard. "Alicent sent a rider to Oldtown under her own seal—not the King's, and not Lord Lyonel's."

Shaera's fingers tightened on the stone parapet. "Why?"

"Because she is afraid," Gwayne said simply. "And when my sister is afraid, she reaches for the only shield she has ever known—our father."

"You stayed away two years, Gwayne."

For a fraction of a second, his right hand shifted on the crossguard of his sword—turning just enough that his little finger brushed the crimson wool of her skirt in the shadow behind the pillar.

"Do you think I stayed in Oldtown because I wished to?" he asked quietly. "After Duskendale, my father asked Lord Hobert why my horse had been seen watering on the Bay of Crabs when my business was in the Reach. One more league north, Shaera, and Otto would have had three eyes watching every shadow you cast in this keep."

Before she could answer, the shouting in the yard turned ugly.

Ser Criston had pitted eldest against eldest—lazy, long-limbed Aegon against twelve-year-old Jacaerys—and was driving Aegon to beat the smaller boy into the sand without mercy. When Ser Harwin Strong strode forward to pull Jace up from the dirt, Criston smiled his thin, poison-sweet smile.

"You have a gentle heart, Ser Harwin," Criston said loudly enough for every squire in the yard to hear. "It is always a mystery to me how a man gives equal attention to every boy... unless he has the interest of a father—or a brother, or a cousin."

With a roar like a baited bear, Harwin Strong lunged.

Steel gauntlets crashed into white enamel. Harwin drove his fist into Criston's jaw, knocked him into the dirt, and fell upon the Kingsguard knight with both fists hammering down while the boys shrieked and men-at-arms plunged into the fray to tear the Commander of the City Watch off the bleeding knight.

"Breakwaters! Hold him!" Lord Lyonel Strong thundered, pelting down the stone steps from the balcony as Viserys stared in dismay.

Every eye in the yard, every guard on the battlements, every stableboy by the troughs surged toward the dust cloud where four men were dragging a bellowing, blood-knuckled Harwin Strong toward the armoury doors.

In the sudden emptiness of the stone arcade, Gwayne's hand closed around Shaera's wrist and drew her three steps back into the dark, vaulted passage behind the shield-racks.

Her back met the cool limestone wall. Then Gwayne was in front of her, bracing one forearm against the stone beside her temple, his chest rising and falling against hers as the roar of the yard echoed twenty paces away.

Up close, fourteen years vanished between one breath and the next. He smelled of the same verbena soap and saddle-leather and Reach sun; the white scar her father's tilt had carved through his brow was three inches from her mouth.

"Tonight," Gwayne whispered fiercely against her lips, his right hand sliding around the curve of her waist as if he had starved for the shape of her ribs through twenty-four moons of silence. "The rookery stair. At the changing of the watch."

"Rhaenyra is speaking to the Small Council at dusk," Shaera breathed, her fingers tangling instinctively in the grey wool at his breast. "After Harwin—she means to leave King's Landing, Gwayne. She told me before I came down. She means to take the boys and go to Dragonstone before the moon turns."

Gwayne stilled against her. His grey-green eyes darkened with a sudden, raw ache.

"And you?" he asked.

"Silverwing and I fly with her."

His jaw tightened. Slowly, his thumb rose to trace the sharp line of her cheekbone, just as he had done when she was five-and-ten and the realm was still whole.

"The rookery stair," Gwayne repeated softly, his mouth brushing the corner of hers for one stolen, reckless fraction of a second before boots clattered at the mouth of the passage. "Even if the castle burns down around us before midnight."

## III. The Country in the Dust

At the eleventh hour, the seventy spiraling steps behind the royal sept were colder than they had been in their youth, though the pigeon feathers and lime-dust lay undisturbed along the narrow gallery connecting the old armoury to the rookery.

When Shaera pushed open the iron-bound postern at the top of the stair, the shutters stood hooked back to the night wind off Blackwater Bay. Far below, the torches of the City Watch traced gold ribbons through the dark streets, and out across the water the moon laid a track of hammered silver toward Dragonstone.

Gwayne stood by the embrasure with his cloak unclasped. He had washed the road-dust from his hair; in the pale light he wore only a dark linen tunic laced with leather cord and his sword-belt slung low at his hip.

As the latch clicked shut behind her, he turned and held out both hands.

Shaera did not stop two paces short of the moonlight as she had at fifteen. She walked straight into his arms, letting her forehead drop against the hollow of his shoulder as his arms came around her back and pulled her tight against his chest.

For a long time neither of them said a word. She breathed in the salt-and-bay-leaf warmth of him, feeling the steady, heavy thud of his heart through two layers of wool and linen while his chin rested atop her silver-gold crown.

"Fourteen years," Gwayne murmured into her hair, his voice rough. "We were children in this dust when your father broke my horse's legs, Shaera. Look at us now."

She tilted her head back to look up at him. Lifting her right hand, she laid the pads of her fingers along the pale, ridged scar that split his left eyebrow and curved down his cheek. "Does it still ache when the rain comes in off the Sound?"

"Only when my father sits across the table from me at the Hightower and reminds me who gave it to me," Gwayne said wryly. He turned his face slightly and pressed his lips into the center of her palm. "And you, my Lady of Runestone? How many suitors from the Vale has King Viserys paraded before you since the autumn?"

"Three," Shaera said, a dry ghost of a smile touching her mouth. "A Belmore with seven chins, a Corbray who was terrified of horses—let alone Silverwing—and my cousin Gerold, who only wanted my hand so he could lock me in the high tower and keep the harbor revenues for himself."

"And you refused them all."

"I told my uncle the King that Silverwing grew restless whenever a man from the Vale came within a bowshot of the pit, and that it would be a great tragedy for the Crown if she ate a lord paramount's nephew."

Gwayne laughed—a low, quiet sound that warmed the cold stone arch—before his eyes grew serious again. His hands slid down her arms to her waist, his thumbs resting against her ribs. "And what of me? Your uncle asked Lord Hobert last winter why the heir to Ser Otto Hightower remained unwed at two-and-thirty."

Shaera's breath caught slightly. "What did you tell him?"

Gwayne reached down to the inner lining of his heavy leather sword-belt. From a narrow stitched seam beside the buckle, he drew out a small folded packet of oiled vellum—the same Citadel vellum she had carried in her sleeve fourteen years ago, returned to him at Duskendale—and unfolded it between them.

Inside lay a single sprig of bay laurel, brittle as spun glass, dried to the color of old bronze, its stem still bearing the tiny dark crescents where a fifteen-year-old girl's fingernails had bitten into it in the royal box.

"I told him nothing," Gwayne whispered. "Because if my father ever knew that for fourteen years I have measured every woman in the Reach against Prince Daemon's daughter—that I have spent half my life loving a woman whose dragon will one day be called to burn my house's banners—he would have me locked in the Citadel vaults before dawn."

The word hung in the dusty moonlight between them, spoken plain at last without the shield of jest or courtesy.

*Loving.*

Not *friends*. Never friends, though they had worn that thin linen lie until it frayed to threads against their skin.

Shaera's hands rose from his chest to frame his face. Her thumbs brushed the silver threads at his temples and the white scar across his cheek. All her life she had been divided between Daemon's Valyrian fire and Rhea Royce's Vale bronze, King's Landing and Runestone. Only between Gwayne Hightower's hands had the two halves of her ever ceased to war.

"Rhaenyra offered Jacaerys's hand to Helaena at the council table tonight," Shaera whispered. "And a dragon's egg from Syrax's clutch for Aemond. She tried to mend the breach before we leave."

Gwayne closed his eyes for a beat, leaning his cheek into her palm. "And Alicent refused."

"She said Rhaenyra's sons were plain-featured insults to the throne. And Lord Lyonel went to the King after the council to resign his badge as Hand and take Harwin back to Harrenhal."

When Gwayne opened his eyes, they were bleak and clear. "Viserys will let Harwin go, even if he keeps Lyonel for a moon. And the moment there is a crack in the door, Alicent will have my father summoned back from Oldtown to take the Hand's chain again." He stepped closer, pressing her gently back against the fluted stone pillar until there was no air left between their bodies. "When you fly to Dragonstone tomorrow, Shaera, the gates will close behind you. We both know it."

"Then do not talk of tomorrow," she breathed.

She rose on her toes, her fingers sliding into the thick chestnut hair at the nape of his neck, and brought her mouth to his.

Fourteen years ago in the chirurgeon's tent, their kiss had been bruised and hesitant, halted by the tolling of a funeral bell. Tonight there were no bells, and neither of them was fifteen anymore.

Gwayne groaned low in his throat, his arms locking around her waist to lift her half an inch off the dusty flagstones as his mouth parted over hers. He tasted of spiced Reach wine and salt air and fourteen years of famine. Shaera kissed him back with all the fierce, banked heat of her blood—her lips parting to the brush of his tongue, her back arching into his palms as his hands slid up her spine through the crimson wool, pulling her so close she felt the iron buckle of his belt and the hard, lean line of his thighs against her skirts.

When he tore his mouth from hers to catch a ragged breath, his lips trailed hot and open-mouthed down the column of her throat, pressing into the hollow of her collarbone where her pulse hammered wild against his teeth.

"Come south with me," Gwayne whispered against her skin, his voice shaking for the first time since she had known him. "Not to Oldtown—to the Honeywine, or across the sea to the Disputed Lands, or anywhere Silverwing can carry two saddles. Let Rhaenyra and Alicent tear the iron chair to pieces between them."

Shaera's eyes were shut, her head tipped back against the cold stone pillar while his hands burned through her bodice. For three dizzying heartbeats she saw it: the white towers of the Reach receding below Silverwing's pale belly, the Narrow Sea opening gold and endless ahead of them, no fathers, no crowns, no bastards or usurpers.

And then she saw Rhaenyra's bleeding feet on the stairs of Maegor's Holdfast, and little Jace and Luke standing small and dark-haired before a court waiting to butcher them. And she saw Gwayne's sister Alicent, trapped in her green velvet cage with three children whose lives Ser Otto had already taught her to fear for.

Slowly, with an effort that felt like drawing a barbed arrowhead out of her own ribs, Shaera brought her hands to Gwayne's shoulders and stilled him.

Her forehead dropped to his. Both of them were breathing hard in the silver dark, their mouths wet and swollen, standing on the razor's edge of the final surrender—still *almost lovers*, held back by the one thing stronger than desire: the bone-deep certainty that neither of them was made of weak enough metal to abandon their blood to the fire.

"You would hate yourself before we reached the Stepstones if you left your sister and her boys to your father's mercy," Shaera whispered against his lips, her thumb brushing his scarred brow. "And I would hate myself before Dragonstone faded astern if I left Rhaenyra alone."

Gwayne let out a long, shuddering breath, his forehead pressing hard against hers as his hands tightened once more around her waist before slowly, agonizingly gentling.

"Damn your Runestone spine," he whispered hoarsely.

"And damn your Oldtown honor," she answered, pressing one last, lingering kiss to the white scar across his cheekbone.

## IV. Ashes Across the Water and the River

Before the ships had even finished loading their casks at the King's Landing wharves, the world began to burn from both ends at once.

The first black-winged raven came across the Narrow Sea from Pentos on the fourth morning after Joffrey's birth, bearing the seal of the Prince of Pentos and a scrap of parchment written in a clerk's shaking hand.

Shaera was in Rhaenyra's solar helping pack the cedar chests with the boys' winter cloaks when Maester Orwyle brought the scroll to the King and the Princess.

Lady Laena Velaryon was dead.

After a day and night of hopeless labor in Pentos, refusing the surgeon's blade that had killed Queen Aemma, Laena had walked out in her bloodied shift to ancient Vhagar and spoken a single word into the dark: *"Dracarys."*

In the silence of the solar, Rhaenyra sank into a chair with her hand over her mouth, weeping for Laenor's sister and her own dear friend.

Shaera stood by the window looking out toward the grey sea, her hands cold at her sides. Across the water, two little girls—her half-sisters Baela and Rhaena, whose faces she had never seen—were standing over a pile of char and bone where their mother had been an hour before. And beside them stood Prince Daemon, twice a widower now: one wife dead by a stone in the Vale, one by dragonfire in Pentos.

*When the rats finish chewing through your uncle's floor,* her father had told her fourteen years ago under the portcullis, *you will need her wings.*

She did not have to wait a sennight to smell the rats.

Three days later, on the eve of Rhaenyra's departure for Dragonstone, Ser Harwin Strong slipped into the Princess's apartments to bid a quiet, heart-wrenching farewell to Jacaerys, Lucerys, and the newborn Joffrey before riding north with his father Lord Lyonel to Harrenhal. Shaera stood guard at the outer door while Harwin kissed the crown of Joffrey's dark head and promised Jace he would return when the weather cooled.

Four mornings after the Strongs rode out through the Gods Gate, a rider on a foundered, foam-flecked mare galloped across the drawbridge of the Red Keep at dawn, shouting for the King.

Harrenhal had burned in the night.

A fire in the old stables and the tower vaults—so the terrified survivors claimed—had trapped Lord Lyonel Strong and his heir Ser Harwin behind bolted oak doors, roasting the Hand of the King and the strongest knight in the Seven Kingdoms alive in their beds.

When the news ran through the holdfast, Shaera locked Rhaenyra's doors for two hours so no courtier could see the Princess drop to her knees beside the cold hearth, muffling her raw sobs for the man she had loved for ten years in the dark.

At dusk, when Rhaenyra had washed her face in cold well-water and put on her black-and-crimson traveling cloak to lead her household down to the docks, Shaera walked alone down the back stair of the holdfast toward the postern that led to the Dragonpit wheelhouse.

As she turned the corner past the Queen's herb garden, where the covered gallery overlooked the private courtyard of the Queen's apartments, a hand reached out from the shadowed archway of the sept stair and drew her behind the buttress.

It was Gwayne.

He was already dressed for the road in his smoke-grey riding leathers, his cloak strapped over his shoulders, yet his face was as pale and grim as the night fourteen years ago when he had watched his sister walk toward the King's bed in their dead mother's dress.

"Look down through the grate," Gwayne whispered, his voice flat with disgust.

Shaera glanced through the carved stone quatrefoil overlooking the Queen's private herb walk. Below, limping unhurriedly along the gravel path away from Queen Alicent's solar with his iron-shod cane tapping softly against the flags, walked Harwin Strong's younger brother—Larys the Clubfoot. Pinned to the lapel of his dark velvet doublet gleamed a small silver badge shaped like an eye within a lotus flower.

"He has been closeted with my sister for an hour," Gwayne said quietly beside her ear. "While the ashes of his father and brother are still smoking in the Riverlands. And five minutes after he limped in, Alicent sent a royal courier out through the River Gate with the King's own seal—summoning our father back from Oldtown to resume the office of Hand."

Shaera turned to stare up at him in the shadow of the buttress, a cold horror coiling in her stomach. "Your sister—did she order Harrenhal burned?"

"I don't know whether she ordered it or whether the Clubfoot laid the corpses at her feet as a gift," Gwayne said, his grey-green eyes hard and sick. "And what freezes my blood, Shaera, is that I am no longer sure which answer is worse."

He reached out and caught her gloved hands in his. Through the soft kidskin, she could feel the callouses of his sword-hand and the steady, desperate pressure of his fingers.

"Listen to me," Gwayne murmured urgently, glancing toward the courtyard where the royal baggage wagons were already rolling toward the Mud Gate and the waiting Velaryon galleys. "Once you reach Dragonstone, do not let Rhaenyra fly back to this keep alone, and do not walk these corridors without Dark Sister's shadow or Silverwing within call. The men my sister and my father are gathering around the throne do not fight with lances in the sun. They fight with locked doors and torches in the fourth watch of the night."

"And you ride south to escort Ser Otto back to his chair," Shaera whispered, the truth of it cutting between them like a drawn wire.

"I ride to meet him on the Roseroad," Gwayne said, "and then I return to Oldtown to take command of the city watch there—as far from Larys Strong and the Red Keep as I can put my sword."

## V. Scale and Stone

An hour before high tide, the wind off Blackwater Bay turned sharp and salt-sweet, filling the black sails of the Velaryon galley moored below the rocky stair of the sea-gate.

Princess Rhaenyra had already gone aboard with Laenor and the three boys, standing at the stern rail with Joffrey held tight against her breast as King Viserys watched from the high parapet above the cliffs, a frail figure in black velvet waving a lone hand after the daughter he did not know how to protect from his own court.

Shaera did not board the ship.

Silverwing was too vast to be left penned in the domes of Rhaenys's Hill while her rider sailed, and Rhaenyra had asked her cousin to fly the high patrol above the galley's masthead across the bay to Dragonstone alongside Syrax and Seasmoke.

Instead of taking the wheelhouse up the hill to the main gates of the Dragonpit, Shaera walked the narrow, sea-carved postern path along the base of the cliff where the old stone smugglers' steps climbed up toward the eastern saddle-yards.

Just inside the sea-gate arch, where the spray from the breakers misted through the iron portcullis and the roar of the tide drowned all sound from the road above, a tall bay horse stood tethered to an iron ring in the rock.

Gwayne stood beside the arch with the reins looped over his arm, the green-lined hood of his traveling cloak thrown back to the salt wind.

They had less time now than they had possessed fourteen years ago; already, high on the crest of the hill, the Dragonkeepers were unchaining the bronze doors of the eastern vault, and Silverwing's deep, trumpeting call rolled down across the water like distant thunder.

Shaera stopped before him on the wet flagstones.

Neither of them knew when—or under what banners—they would ever stand within reach of each other's hands again. Fourteen years had passed between King Viserys's naming of his heir and this grey morning by the sea; before another fourteen passed, the frail old King on the parapet would be cold upon a pyre of his own, and the realm would be asked to pay in blood for every secret kept in the dark.

Shaera unfastened the small leather pouch at her girdle. From within it, she drew out a single dragon-scale no larger than the palm of her hand—shed from the curve of Silverwing's throat after their flight over the bay, thin as hammered armour-plate, shimmering with the pale, indestructible silver of Old Valyria.

With her bronze Royce dagger, she had pierced a tiny hole near the upper edge and threaded it with a cord of braided black horsehair.

She held it out to him.

"You have carried dead leaves in your belt for fourteen years, Ser Gwayne," Shaera said softly over the crash of the surf. "Carry something that does not burn."

Gwayne's throat worked. Slowly, he took the silver scale from her palm. Even in the grey shadow of the sea-gate, it caught the light off the whitecaps like a shard of fallen moon. He slipped the black cord over his head, tucking the cool Valyrian scale beneath the collar of his linen tunic so that it lay flat against the skin above his heart, right beside the silver watchtower clasp of his cloak.

"And what have I left to give a lady who rides a queen's dragon and owns a castle in the Vale?" Gwayne asked, his voice rough and low.

"Your word," Shaera said, looking straight into his grey-green eyes, "that if the sky ever turns red above the Reach, you will look up before you raise your shield."

Gwayne stepped forward, closing the single pace of wet stone between them. His ungloved hands came up to frame her face, his thumbs resting against her cheekbones as he looked down at her as if memorizing every silver lash and every stubborn line of her mouth against a winter that might last the rest of their lives.

"If Silverwing ever comes over the Honeywine," Gwayne whispered, his forehead coming down to rest against hers, "she will find no lance raised in my hand against her rider. Not for my father. Not for a crown. Not for all the Seven Kingdoms."

He tilted her chin up and kissed her—once, deep and fierce and unhurried in the salt spray of the sea-gate, a kiss that tasted of rain and iron and fourteen years of unbroken faith. His mouth lingered against hers for one final, aching heartbeat, and then he let his lips brush the bare pulse at the inside of her right wrist, just where he had kissed her when they were young and the peace still held.

"Fly safe, Shaera," Gwayne whispered against her skin.

"Ride well, Ser Gwayne," she answered.

She turned and climbed the stone steps into the wind without looking back.

Half an hour later, when the iron shoe of Ser Gwayne Hightower's charger struck the cobbles of the Roseroad outside the River Gate, a shadow broader than a ship's sail passed across the sun above the mud and the watchtowers.

Gwayne reined his horse to a halt at the crest of the southern hill, one hand rising to rest over the cool silver scale beneath his tunic as he looked up into the glare.

Three thousand feet above Blackwater Bay, where the smoke of King's Landing could not reach, Silverwing banked wide into the eastern wind—her pale wings flashing once like a drawn blade in the morning sun before she turned her head toward Dragonstone and vanished into the clouds.
