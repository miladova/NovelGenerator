# The Weight of Unasked Favors

*A House of the Dragon One-Shot*

---

## I. Silver Over the Blackwater

High above the smoke of King's Landing, the cold had no smell of men in it.

Below the clouds lay a smear of tallow, mud, and charred timber, yet three thousand feet above the Blackwater Rush there was only salt wind and morning glare. Lady Shaera Targaryen leaned over the high pommel of her saddle, gloved knuckles locked around the straps riveted into Silverwing's pale dorsal plates as the she-dragon's ribs expanded beneath her like the hull of a war galley.

Fifty yards down the wind, Syrax cut an insolent arc through the vapor. The younger dragon's scales flashed the color of new-minted gold as Princess Rhaenyra sent her diving toward the slate dome of the Dragonpit.

*"Āeksio!"* Shaera called into the rush of air. Good Queen Alysanne's mount was older and calmer in the bone; a shift of Shaera's knee against the scarred shoulder was enough. Silverwing folded her vast wings and dropped after her golden cousin in a whistling stoop.

The hill of Rhaenys rushed up to meet them. Syrax struck the sanded apron first, skidding on hind claws while Rhaenyra laughed and unbuckled her harness. Silverwing came down with the weight of an avalanche, talons gouging the earth as white-robed Dragonkeepers hurried forward with their staves.

*"Dohaerās, Silverwing,"* the elder keeper chanted, striking his staff twice against the flags. *"Nykēla, zaldrīzes."*

Silverwing exhaled a plume of grey vapor smelling of sulfur and roasted marrow, lowering her horned head so Shaera could swing her leg over the cantle and slide down to the sand.

"You held her back on the turn over Claw Isle," Rhaenyra accused, striding across the courtyard as she tugged off her gauntlets. Her silver-gold braids were coming loose at the temples, her cheeks whipped pink. "I saw you. You let me take the seaward reach."

"Syrax was straining like a gull after a herring," Shaera replied, unfastening her riding cloak. "Silverwing's wing-wash would have tumbled you halfway to Driftmark."

Rhaenyra snorted, linking her arm through Shaera's as they walked out beneath the portcullis toward the waiting wheelhouse. "My father has no thoughts to spare for saddles today. He has scarcely slept for pacing outside the birthing chamber."

At the foot of the steps, beside the gilded carriage bearing the three-headed dragon, Lady Alicent Hightower stood in a gown of sky-blue wool, her right thumb working anxiously against the torn skin around her left index nail.

"Have you been riding again?" Alicent asked the instant they came within earshot, her dark eyes darting from Rhaenyra's wind-tangled hair to the saddle-grease on Shaera's cuff.

"Everywhere I looked, there was only sky," Rhaenyra said dreamily, catching Alicent's hands before the Hand's daughter could curtsy.

"You both smell of the pit," Alicent murmured, as the footmen handed them into the wheelhouse. Once the horses lurched onto the cobbles, Alicent looked across at Shaera. "Septa Marlow asked for you twice. And... the baggage trains from the Reach finished unloading an hour after dawn."

Shaera kept her gaze fixed on the carriage window. Not Highgarden's roses, for the Tyrells had pitched their tents three days past. She meant the green-and-grey wagons bearing the white watchtower crowned in flame.

"Your brother rides in the lists tomorrow, does he not?" Rhaenyra asked idly, leaning back against the cushions. "Your father must be pleased to have his heir at court."

"Gwayne is always pleased to leave Oldtown when the autumn rains begin," Alicent said, her thumb returning to her ragged cuticle. "Though my father says a tourney is no place for vanity, only a proving of discipline."

"Your father thinks drawing breath is a proving of discipline," Shaera said dryly.

Rhaenyra laughed, and even Alicent ducked her chin. They had grown up together in Maegor's Holdfast—Rhaenyra the realm's darling, Alicent the Hand's dutiful shadow, and Shaera, the awkward sum neither King's Landing nor Runestone knew how to reckon.

She was five-and-ten now, born of the single winter night Prince Daemon Targaryen had endured beneath the roof of Runestone before swearing that the ewes of the Vale had sweeter faces than his bride. When she was six, King Viserys had summoned her from the Vale to be raised beside his daughter. From Daemon, Shaera had taken silver-gold hair, sharp cheekbones, and violet eyes; from Lady Rhea Royce she had inherited an unbending spine and a bronze runic dagger kept hidden at the bottom of her chest.

When the wheelhouse rumbled into the Red Keep, Shaera stepped down into the churn of mud and shouting squires. Beyond the outer gate, her gaze caught on a bay charger led by a boy in grey-and-green livery, its saddle cloth stitched with the white beacon tower. Her pulse gave a traitorous kick against her collarbone.

Queen Aemma's apartments were stifling, banked with red charcoal at Mellos's command. The Queen lay propped on her oak couch, her belly rising beneath her shift like a burial mound.

"You smell of dragon, the pair of you," Aemma said, holding out one swollen hand. Rhaenyra knelt beside the couch while Shaera poured lemonwater. Aemma took the cup with a fond nod—she too carried the blood of the Vale. "This is the last time I shall ever be brought to bed," the Queen murmured, shifting with a low, involuntary groan. "We have royal wombs, you and I. Both of you. The childbed is our battlefield. We must learn to face it with a stiff lip."

"I would rather serve as a knight and ride to battle and glory," Rhaenyra said, her chin lifting.

When they left the Queen to sleep, Rhaenyra hurried ahead toward the bathhouse before pouring wine for the Small Council, while Shaera carried Aemma's empty phials down the gallery flanking the Great Hall.

The huge oak-and-bronze doors stood ajar. Court would not sit until past noon, yet a lone figure sat sprawled across the Iron Throne.

There was no mistaking the long legs booted in black leather, the gold cloak pooled over the iron steps, or the hand resting on Dark Sister. Rhaenyra had just passed through; the velvet pouch from her new Valyrian steel necklace still lay on the third step.

*"Ziry māzis,"* Prince Daemon murmured in High Valyrian. *She comes.* "And here I thought only the little princess had the stomach to fly before noon. The keepers tell me Silverwing took three sheep at breaking fast."

Shaera stopped at the base of the steps and inclined her head. "Father. We did not know you had returned to the Red Keep."

"Your uncle's council prefers it when I do not announce myself," Daemon said, descending the iron blades with predatory grace. He stopped two steps above her, catching a loose strand of silver-gold hair at her temple. "Your lady mother sent another raven to the King last sennight. Not a word of you in six lines of parchment, though she reminded him that my seat at court deprives the Vale of my sword."

Shaera neither flinched nor leaned into his touch. "My mother has little need of swords in the autumn, Father. And she knows I am well kept here."

"Here," Daemon echoed softly, glancing toward the doors leading to the Tower of the Hand. "In Otto Hightower's nursery." "The Hand's eldest whelp rode in at dawn," Daemon said, his hand tightening on her shoulder. "Gwayne, they call him. All polished plate and Oldtown oil. Keep clear of their pavilions tomorrow, Shaera."

Shaera kept her violet eyes locked on the silver dragon pinned to his breastplate. "I sit in the royal box with Rhaenyra, Father. I have no business among the Reachmen's tents."

Daemon studied her for a beat, then tapped her chin with the back of two fingers. "Good. One Hightower leeching onto a Targaryen girl in this keep is more than enough."

## II. The Gallery Above the Armoury

That night, King's Landing bled.

Shaera heard the boots of the Gold Cloaks marching out at dusk—two thousand strong in their new black breastplates and long golden cloaks. By moonrise, the wind carried the roar of Flea Bottom and the clatter of carts rolling toward the gates heaped with severed hands and ears.

At the eleventh hour, Shaera drew a dark cloak over her gown and slipped up the seventy spiraling steps behind the sept to the dusty stone gallery connecting the old armoury to the rookery.

He was already waiting when she reached the top of the stair.

Moonlight slanted through a hooked shutter where Ser Gwayne Hightower leaned against a stone pillar, his green cloak thrown back over a travel-creased doublet as he turned a small autumn pear in his ungloved hands.

In the great hall Gwayne looked every inch his house: tall, chestnut-haired, and composed. Here in the half-dark, where no father watched, the stiffness fell from his shoulders, leaving a wry warmth in his grey-green eyes.

"Lady Shaera," he murmured, executing a bow so absurdly low his cloak brushed the pigeon dust. "I had begun to fear the ravens had eaten you on the stairs."

"The ravens have too much sense to nest near your father's tower," Shaera said, pushing back her hood. Something tight behind her breastbone loosened the instant she saw him whole. Six moons had passed since he had ridden south to Oldtown—six moons of reading his sideways postscripts tucked at the bottom of Alicent's family letters.

Gwayne's gaze traveled over her face with a quiet thoroughness that warmed the skin at her throat. "Look at you," he said softly, all mockery gone. "You still have wind-burn across your nose. You flew today."

"Above the bay. With Rhaenyra." She stepped closer, stopping just short of the bar of moonlight falling through the shutter. "You are late arriving."

"I was detained at Bitterbridge by three flooded fords," Gwayne said, drawing a bone-handled eating knife from his belt and slicing a crescent of the pear to hold out to her on the flat of the blade, "and a letter from my father reminding me four times that the honor of Oldtown rests upon the straightness of my lance."

Shaera did not take the fruit with her fingers; she leaned forward and took it straight from the flat of his knife with her lips, her violet eyes never leaving his.

Gwayne's breath caught—just enough that his hand stilled and the silver blade gleamed between them. The pear tasted of honey and southern orchards.

"And how is the honor of Oldtown tonight?" she asked quietly.

"Severely imperiled," Gwayne murmured, lowering the knife, "given that I have spent the last hour standing in bird-lime waiting for Prince Daemon's daughter while my squire believes I am at prayer."

"Wait until you hear what my father has been doing while you prayed." She nodded toward the red glow of torches moving down the lanes below. "He took the Gold Cloaks into the alleys at sundown. Half the dungeons were emptied onto the block before moonrise."

Gwayne's face sobered. "My father came back from the Small Council an hour ago with a face like carved salt. And what did the Prince say to you?"

"He told me in the throne room at noon that you are all polished plate and Oldtown oil, and ordered me not to go near your pavilions."

A slow, crooked smile touched Gwayne's mouth. "He noticed my plate?"

"He noticed your name," Shaera said, all humor fading. She reached out, her fingers closing around the linen cuff at his wrist where his pulse beat hard against her thumb. "Be careful of him tomorrow, Gwayne. He is in a mood to break something that belongs to the King or the Hand."

Slowly, as if moving toward a hawk that might bolt at the snap of a twig, Gwayne turned his palm up and let his ungloved fingers slide across the inside of her wrist.

Since colliding two years past in the upper stacks of the royal library, they had built a secret country out of what they did not do. To the court, they had spoken perhaps five times in their lives. None knew that he kept a feather shed by Silverwing stitched inside his shield-arm padding, or that when Queen Aemma's last babe died in the cradle, Gwayne had sat with Shaera behind the bell-ropes until dawn.

They were friends. That was the word they used when they dared use any word at all—a flimsy timber barrier set between two charging horses.

Yet now, as Gwayne stepped half a pace closer into the shadow of the pillar, his fingers slid from her wrist up her sleeve to the curve of her elbow, and then higher, until his warm palm came to rest against the side of her neck just below her ear.

Shaera's eyelids fluttered shut. Her hand rose to his quilted doublet, her fingers curling into the linen over his heart. It was hammering as fast and unguarded as her own.

"I have ridden in twenty tourneys, Shaera," Gwayne whispered against her parted lips, his thumb brushing her cheekbone. "I know how to take a lance on the shield."

"You do not know how he rides when he hates the man in the box," she breathed. When she opened her eyes, his face was an inch away. He smelled of verbena soap, crushed bay leaf, and the salt heat of the road.

His forehead rested against hers. When he shifted, his mouth grazed the corner of hers—soft, dry, a question asked without sound. One fraction of an inch remained; one turn of her head and the word *friend* would be ash between them.

Then, from the courtyard below the bell tower, came the clank of a halberd butt striking the cobbles and the harsh laugh of two Gold Cloaks changing the midnight post.

Neither jumped apart, yet the invisible iron of their names slipped back between their ribs like a blade into its scabbard. *Targaryen. Hightower. Daemon. Otto.*

Slowly, Gwayne pressed his lips—not to her mouth, where ruin lay, but to the hollow of her temple, holding them there for one fierce second before drawing back.

"Look for me when I ride out," he murmured, pressing a folded square of vellum into her palm. "Not with your eyes—only know that if I carried a favor tomorrow, it would not be green."

Closing her fingers over it, he stepped back into the dark stairwell.

## III. The Lance and the Laurel

By mid-morning the tourney grounds outside the city walls looked as though all the wildflowers of the Reach and the Riverlands had burst into bloom across the mud.

Beneath the black-and-red canopy of the royal box, King Viserys sat beaming with Ser Otto Hightower at his elbow, while Rhaenyra sat in the front row between Alicent and Shaera behind a rail draped in bay laurel.

"Be welcome!" Viserys called out, rising with his hands spread wide as the lists fell quiet. "I do not stand before you today as your King, my lords, my ladies, but as a father—awaiting the birth of his heir! The Queen is in her labors as we speak! And may the luck of the Seven shine upon all combatants!"

No sooner had the King retaken his seat than the trumpets blared, and all pretense of courtly gentleness vanished in a spray of red mud and teeth. Knights shaped by the Long Peace swung morningstars and poleaxes with bottled savagery. When a Baratheon knight caved in an opponent's faceplate with a warhammer, Alicent flinched beside Rhaenyra, tearing at her cuticle.

"It shouldn't be allowed," Alicent whispered through white lips.

"Lord Corlys says these knights are green as summer grass," Rhaenyra replied, leaning forward with her chin in her hand. "None of them have seen real war."

At the end of the bench, Shaera sat with her back straight as a lance-shaft, her hands folded over the wreath of dark laurel leaves in her lap. Inside her crimson sleeve pressed the vellum Gwayne had given her—a chart of Valyrian dragon-roads copied from the Citadel's vaults.

On the sand below, a young knight in plain black mail—Ser Criston Cole of Blackhaven, son of Lord Dondarrion's steward—unhorsed both Baratheon cousins in rapid succession, earning a curious glance from Rhaenyra.

Then the great bronze horns at the western gate sounded three long blasts.

From the crimson-and-black pavilion at the head of the lists rode Prince Daemon Targaryen. Armored in overlapping black dragon-scale and his winged helm, Dark Sister at his hip, Daemon carried a twelve-foot lance painted the color of dried blood.

"The Prince," the herald shouted above the din, "by grace of His Majesty, has the right to choose his first opponent!"

Five shields hung along the barrier before the royal stand: Baratheon, Mooton, Mallister, Frey—and nearest the box, a smoke-grey shield emblazoned with the white watchtower crowned in green flame.

Beneath that banner sat Ser Gwayne Hightower in mirror-polished Oldtown steel, a green silk mantle streaming from his pauldrons. True to his word, he did not glance once toward the corner of the box where Shaera sat; his grey-green eyes remained fixed straight down the lists.

Slowly, deliberately, Daemon walked his black stallion down the line of shields. Past Mallister. Past Frey. Past Mooton.

As his charger's hooves thudded directly beneath the royal box, Daemon's lilac eyes lifted. He looked first at Ser Otto Hightower; then his gaze flicked half a span to the left—sweeping over Rhaenyra, over Alicent, and for one razor-sharp instant brushing Shaera's pale face.

Daemon lowered the iron point of his black lance and struck the watchtower shield of Ser Gwayne Hightower with a ringing *crack*.

"Ser Gwayne Hightower of Oldtown!" the herald bellowed. "Eldest son of the Hand of the King!"

Beside Rhaenyra, Alicent gasped. One row back, Otto Hightower's knuckles turned the color of tallow against his chair. Shaera did not move; only beneath the folds of her crimson skirt, her fingernails bit into the laurel leaves in her lap until the crushed stems stained her palms with green sap.

Down on the sand, Gwayne saluted the King, reached up with one mailed hand, and snapped his visor shut.

The trumpets blew.

Hooves thundered against the packed earth as black dragon-scale and silvered steel hurtled toward each other along opposite sides of the central barrier. At the midpoint of the tilt, Gwayne shifted his shield half an inch to meet Daemon's point.

*CRACK.*

Both lances shattered into painted splinters. Gwayne rocked back in his high cantle from the force of the blow, yet he kept his stirrups and rode out the pass while the commons roared.

"Well ridden!" Viserys called out, clapping his hands before glancing at his Hand. "Your boy has a strong seat, Otto!"

On the second pass, Daemon spurred harder against the barrier. Again the lances shattered into white splinters against shield and dragon crest; again Gwayne held his saddle.

In the front row, Alicent let out a shaky breath. "He matches him," she whispered. "Three passes is a fair tilt—"

"My uncle does not ride for fair tilts," Rhaenyra murmured.

Shaera's heart hammered in her ears. She had watched her father practice in the lower yard since she was seven; she knew the exact way his left shoulder dipped when he grew tired of a game and decided to end it in blood.

As the heralds blew for the third pass, Daemon took a heavier ash lance from his squire. The black stallion lunged forward, foam flying from its bit. From the opposite end, Gwayne spurred his bay charger into the run, his shield raised high to guard his throat.

Thirty yards apart. Twenty. Ten.

Then Shaera saw her father's right elbow drop.

He did not aim for the shield. In the final heartbeat before impact, Daemon dropped the iron coronel of his black lance two feet below the tilt rail—straight into the galloping forelegs of Gwayne Hightower's horse.

The bay charger screamed as nine feet of solid ash snapped its shins at full gallop.

Horse and rider cartwheeled across the splintering barrier. Gwayne was flung headlong over the pommel, hitting the packed earth shoulder-first; his helm tore loose on the second bounce as he skidded through gravel and broken wood before lying still.

"Gwayne!" Alicent shrieked, half-rising from her bench as the crowd erupted into a savage, baying uproar. Behind them, Ser Otto stood up so sharply his chair scraped backward across the planks.

Shaera sat with her spine locked like Runestone bronze, a drop of blood welling where her teeth pierced her lower lip. Only when Gwayne's gauntlet twitched and he groaned under the hands of the rushing squires did air return to her lungs.

Without a glance at the litter bearing Gwayne away, Daemon trotted his stallion to the royal box, lifted his visor with a wolfish smile, and poised a fresh lance before Lady Alicent Hightower.

"Good fortune to you, my prince," Alicent whispered, her hands shaking so violently she could scarcely lift her laurel wreath over the iron point while her brother was carried bleeding through the gate behind him.

Daemon inclined his head in mock gallantry, slid Alicent's wreath down his lance, and winked up at Otto Hightower before wheeling his charger back toward the lists.

## IV. Stitches and Smoke

An hour later, the sun had dropped behind the red towers of Maegor's Holdfast, and everything was coming apart at once.

A runner from Grand Maester Mellos had summoned the King and Ser Otto to Queen Aemma's perilous childbed just as Ser Criston Cole knocked Dark Sister from Daemon's hand in the melee below.

As Rhaenyra cast her laurel wreath down to Ser Criston, Shaera slipped through the rear curtains, telling her cousin she meant to fetch Queen Aemma's prayer beads.

Instead, moving swiftly along the muddy lane behind the smiths' stalls where charcoal smoke hung thick, she skirted the outer ring of pavilions until she reached the rear pasture reserved for the Reach baggage wagons.

Behind the empty Hightower pavilion, a lantern burned inside a small canvas surgery tent. Checking the lane twice, Shaera ducked under the rear flap.

The tent smelled of vinegar and crushed yarrow. Bloody shears and horsehair thread lay on a low table; the chirurgeon had already finished his stitching and gone to tend another knight.

On a cedar campaign chest at the back of the tent sat Ser Gwayne Hightower.

Stripped to his sweat-darkened arming tunic, Gwayne sat with the left side of his face swollen purple where five black stitches closed a ragged gash across his brow and cheek, pressing a bloodied clout to his split lip.

At the rustle of the canvas flap, he lowered the cloth and looked up with his one unswollen eye. For a heartbeat he stared at her as if the blow to his head had conjured a ghost. Then he tried to stand.

"Shaera—Seven hells, what are you—" He winced as his bruised ribs caught him, one hand bracing against the tent pole. "If my father's guards come back—"

"Your father is in Maegor's Holdfast with the King, and your guards are drinking sour ale by the horse-troughs," Shaera said, her voice low and trembling so badly she had to clench her jaw. She crossed the three paces of packed dirt between them and pushed him back onto the chest by both shoulders. "Sit down, you absolute fool."

Gwayne sank down, his breath hissing through his teeth, though even with half his face stitched like a sailmaker's sack, the corner of his mouth tried to curve. "Is that how they teach highborn ladies in the Red Keep to comfort the fallen?"

"Comfort you?" She knelt before him, pouring Myrish firewine from a silver flask onto clean cambric with shaking hands. "You broke two lances with him! Every knight in the realm knows a third pass against Daemon ends on the surgeon's table!"

"I am the Hand's son," Gwayne said quietly. When she lifted the wine-soaked cambric to the dried blood along his jaw, just below the fresh stitches, he caught a sharp breath at the sting yet did not pull back an inch. "He struck my shield before the whole realm, Shaera. What would you have had me do? Throw down my lance and beg the Rogue Prince's pardon?"

"I would have had you live!" The words tore out of her throat in a raw whisper. She wiped the dried blood beneath his chin. "I saw his elbow drop before the rail, and I had to sit beside your sister and your father with my hands in my lap while he tried to break your neck—"

Her voice broke. A single hot tear spilled over her lashes and tracked down her cheek.

Gwayne reached up and closed his bruised, calloused fingers around both of her wrists.

"Hey," he murmured. "Look at me."

She looked at him through a blur of violet and silver.

"He broke a fine gelding's legs and gave me a scar for Oldtown supper tables," Gwayne said softly, his thumbs stroking her wrists. "That is all he did."

"And then he rode to the box," Shaera whispered bitterly, "and asked Alicent for her wreath."

A shadow crossed Gwayne's good eye—clean, protective anger for his sister. "Of course he did. He wasn't tilting against me, Shaera. He was tilting against my father, and using our bodies for the barrier."

Slowly, Gwayne released one of her wrists and lifted his hand to her face. His knuckles were skinned raw from his gauntlet, yet his touch against her cheek was almost unbearably gentle. With the pad of his thumb, he wiped the tear beneath her eye, then brushed the swollen inner edge of her lower lip where she had bitten it in the stands.

"You bled too," he whispered.

"It is nothing."

"It is not nothing to me."

He leaned down until his forehead rested against hers. Shaera let the cloth fall, her hands framing the uninjured side of his jaw as a delayed shudder ran through his bruised shoulders.

"When the horse went over," Gwayne breathed against her mouth, "the last thing I saw was your crimson sleeve. I thought—Seven forgive me—if I died in that mud without ever knowing what your mouth tasted like, I would haunt this castle until the stones fell into the sea."

A breathless sound escaped her—half laugh, half sob. She tilted her chin up.

His lips touched hers.

It was not the reckless kiss of a song, for his lip was split and her mouth was raw, and both knew the cost of what they touched. It was slow, aching, and fever-warm—the brush of his mouth against hers, the taste of salt and Myrish wine, crossing the line by a hair's breadth while still poised upon its edge.

His hand slid from her cheek to her throat, his thumb resting over the frantic beat of her pulse, his mouth parting against hers as if to deepen the kiss into something neither could ever walk back from—

*BONG.*

The sound rolled down from the high watchtowers of the Red Keep above the hill—deep, brazen, and slow.

*BONG.*

Gwayne froze against her lips. Both of them knew the bells of King's Landing. Two fast peals meant a royal birth. Three meant a prince.

The great bronze bell of the castle sept tolled a single, solitary note, waited five long heartbeats in the salt wind, and tolled again.

The mourning bell.

Shaera's hands went cold against Gwayne's neck. Slowly she drew back an inch, staring into his grey-green eyes as the fifth and sixth strokes rolled out across the silent pavilions.

"The Queen," Shaera whispered.

## V. Ash, Bone, and the Divided Realm

On the third day after the tournament, the wind off Blackwater Bay smelled of burned cedar and human fat.

Upon a wind-scoured headland above the sea, two shapes lay wrapped in coarse linen atop an oak pyre: Queen Aemma, and nestled in the crook of her arm, a bundle no longer than a quiver.

*Baelon,* they had named him for the six hours he had drawn breath after Grand Maester Mellos's knife took him from his mother's womb.

Shaera stood two paces behind Rhaenyra in a gown of mourning black. High overhead Silverwing wheeled in pale, silent rings, while golden Syrax crouched upon the limestone ridge behind the pyre.

Across the stone semicircle stood Ser Otto Hightower and his household. Gwayne stood behind his father in mourning black, the stitches stark against his cheek; neither he nor Shaera dared look across the torches.

King Viserys stood before the pyre with a torch shaking in his hand, his face hollowed out with grief. When he could not bring himself to give the command, Otto Hightower stepped forward to steady the King's elbow.

Rhaenyra did not wait for the Hand. She stepped past her father and stopped beside Prince Daemon at the cliff's edge. Even from three paces back, Shaera heard the quiet hiss of their High Valyrian over the surf.

*"They are waiting for you,"* Daemon murmured to his niece.

*"I wonder if, during those few hours my brother lived, my father finally found happiness,"* Rhaenyra replied in the old tongue, her voice flat as slate.

*"Your father needs you now more than he ever has."*

*"I will never be a son."*

Daemon glanced once from Rhaenyra's profile to Shaera standing just behind her—his two silver-haired girls, one the King's blood, one his own—before Rhaenyra turned to the ridge above the pyre, lifted her chin, and spoke a single word into the wind.

*"Dracarys."*

Syrax uncoiled her long golden neck, crept down the sheer rock, and opened her jaws. A torrent of orange-and-gold dragonfire roared over the oak timbers, catching the pitch in a blinding sheet of heat while high overhead Silverwing answered with a long, trumpeting cry that echoed out across the Narrow Sea.

Two nights after the pyre, as Shaera walked the dark colonnade toward Maegor's Holdfast, a shadow stepped from an embrasure near the Tower of the Hand.

"Wait."

She knew the voice before she saw the gleam of the stitches along his cheek. Shaera stepped into the deep shadow behind the pillar as Gwayne drew her out of sight of the bridge guards. His jaw was clenched hard, his grey-green eyes dark and sick as he stared past her toward the high, lit windows of the King's private apartments across the drawbridge.

"What is it?" she whispered, her hand going to his sleeve.

"Ten minutes ago, I met Alicent on the stairs of our tower," Gwayne said, each word bitten off like iron wire. "She was coming out of my father's solar with a book of Valyrian histories in her hands, wearing our dead mother's green velvet gown, and walking toward the King's chambers."

The air froze in her lungs: King Viserys, his wife's ashes scarcely cold, and Ser Otto, already moving his pieces across the board.

"No," Shaera breathed. "Alicent is Rhaenyra's closest friend—"

"And my father is the Hand," Gwayne said bitterly. "She wouldn't even look at me—she does not know how to disobey him." His hand rose to cup Shaera's jaw. "And Otto brought a report from Flea Bottom at dusk. Daemon was heard in a Street of Silk brothel toasting *'the Heir for a Day.'* The King will disinherit him at dawn and name Rhaenyra Princess of Dragonstone."

They stood chest to chest in the dark colonnade while the torches flared and guttered in the wind below. Her father's reckless, cruel tongue; his father's patient, bottomless ambition; and between those two millstones, a girl with silver hair and a knight with a stitched cheek who had no business breathing the same air.

"I ride for Oldtown two hours after the investiture," Gwayne whispered. "My father wants me back at the Hightower with Lord Hobert."

"When will you return?"

Gwayne did not lie to her. That had always been the first law of the country they had built in the dust of the upper galleries: they lied to the King, to the Hand, to the Rogue Prince, to the whole blind court, but never to each other.

"I don't know," he whispered.

In the grey light after dawn, following a furious quarrel behind the closed doors of the throne room in which King Viserys ordered his brother back to Runestone and declared Rhaenyra his sole heir, Prince Daemon stormed down the steps toward the outer stables.

Shaera intercepted him under the portcullis, where Daemon stood dressed for the sky in black boiled leather, his jaw white with rage.

"Pack a saddle-bag," Daemon snapped without preamble. "Nothing you cannot strap behind Silverwing's shoulders. Caraxes and I fly at the tide."

Shaera stood very still. "To Runestone?"

Daemon's lip curled. "To Dragonstone, or the Vale, or the ends of the Narrow Sea—anywhere the air doesn't stink of sheep and Hightowers. Your uncle means to put a girl on the Iron Throne while Otto Hightower holds the strings under her skirts. Come, Shaera. You are my blood, not theirs."

Part of her ached to take her father's hand and fly above the smoke. Then she thought of Rhaenyra left alone in the keep, and of the Citadel vellum hidden in her chest.

"Rhaenyra has no mother left in this keep, Father," Shaera said quietly. "If I leave her now, she has no one of our blood beside her at all."

Daemon stared at her, his violet eyes narrowing. "She chose the crown over us the moment she let Viserys name her."

"She did not ask for it," Shaera said, lifting her chin with every ounce of Runestone bronze in her bones. "Any more than I asked to be left between you and the King."

A strange, bleak look passed behind Daemon's eyes—not anger, almost respect, though he would have cut out his tongue before calling it that. *"Your mother's daughter,"* Daemon muttered softly in High Valyrian, swinging up into his saddle. *"Keep Silverwing saddled, little dragon. When the rats finish chewing through your uncle's floor, you will need her wings."*

Two days later, under a thousand candles in the Great Hall, the lords of the realm—Stark, Baratheon, Velaryon, Blackwood—knelt one by one to swear fealty to Rhaenyra, Princess of Dragonstone.

Shaera stood beside the royal dais in Targaryen crimson and black. Across the aisle, beneath the banner of the Hand, stood Lady Alicent with her eyes cast down at the flagstones, and behind her, dressed for the road in traveling leathers and a heavy riding cloak, stood Ser Gwayne.

Only when the great doors were flung wide for the feast and the courtyard filled with departing retinues did two figures slip away from the press on the gallery stairs. They had three minutes—perhaps less, for the Hightower outriders were already mounted by the River Gate.

In the dusty half-light of the rookery arch, where a single shaft of late afternoon sun slanted through the open shutter, Gwayne turned as she reached the top step.

Shaera walked straight into the circle of his cloak. From the inner pocket of her velvet sleeve, she drew out a single sprig of bay laurel—crushed, dried to the color of old olive wood, its stem still stained dark where her nails had bitten into it in the royal box while his horse fell across the barrier.

Without a word, she slid the dried laurel beneath the silver watchtower clasp at his shoulder, tucking it inside the folds of his traveling cloak where no eye on the Roseroad would ever find it.

Gwayne looked down at the crushed green leaf against his chest, then up into her face. His throat worked as he swallowed.

"You kept it," he whispered.

"You told me to look for you," she said softly. "I owed you a favor."

He reached out with both ungloved hands and took hers. Far out over Blackwater Bay, visible through the narrow stone window behind his shoulder, a pair of red wings—Caraxes, carrying her father and his white-cloaked paramour toward the Narrow Sea—dwindled into a dark speck against the eastern clouds. Down in the yard below them, the Hightower trumpets sounded the call to mount.

The world was already dividing itself into black and green, dragon and tower, fire and stone. Soon enough their fathers would demand that every soul in the Seven Kingdoms choose a banner and draw a blade.

In the dust of the high gallery, Gwayne turned her hand over, bowed his stitched head, and pressed his mouth to the bare skin inside her wrist where her pulse beat fast and hot. He held his lips there for three slow heartbeats—not quite a lover's claim, infinitely more than a friend's farewell—until the horn blew a second time below.

"Fly safe, Shaera," Gwayne whispered against her skin.

"Ride well, Ser Gwayne," she answered.

When she opened her eyes, only the dust motes turned in the shaft of autumn sun, and down in the courtyard the iron shoes of the Oldtown horses struck sparks from the stones as they rode out through the gate.
