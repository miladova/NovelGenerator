# The Linden in June

*Warsaw, 1943*

---

The milk queue had formed before dawn and was gone by seven. Marianna Frontczak stood in it with her daughter Alicja and her son Wojtek, keeping the place with her body while the other queue-members complicated it — this way and that, in and out of the cold shadow of the tenement wall, chasing rumors of a cart that might not come.

Forty-two years old. She no longer thought of herself as anything but the shape left by a war: a number on a ration card, a widow's category, two hundred grams of bread a day, a spoonful of barley coffee that tasted of burnt acorns. The card itself lived in her handbag inside a plastic sleeve she had mended twice, and beside it, in a flat tin box that had once held loose tea, she kept the only other papers that mattered in this life: her registration card, the *meldekarte*, Jozef's death certificate, the children's birth certificates, and, at the bottom of everything, a photograph of a shop that no longer existed. She carried it all everywhere. There was nowhere safe to leave paper. Paper was precious; paper was proof you had existed.

Alicja, fifteen, read while they waited. She had grown too thin, Marianna thought, the way city girls grew thin if the war went on long enough — the way they grew themselves hollow, deliberately, to be lighter, to carry less back from the shops. The bones of her wrists showed. She went to school — or to what passed for school now, the komplety, the secret classes held in private apartments, front rooms with the curtains drawn, teacher paid in bread and silence. Polish children were forbidden an education above the elementary. So they learned in whispers, Alicja at the kitchen table with her books stacked under a tablecloth, studying for examinations that produced no certificate, only the terrible private satisfaction of knowing they had not been beaten. She was already fluent in forbidden history, forbidden geography, forbidden poetry. Marianna was proud of her and could not say the word out loud.

Wojtek, nine, swung her hand. He had his father's chin — a strong jaw that hadn't grown in yet, which made it look like determination. In truth he was sweet, easily frightened, brave about small things. He carried his tin cup tied to his belt with a string he always forgot and always wore anyway. He believed, with the unshakeable misunderstanding of children, that if you were quiet enough and clean enough, the soldiers would look through you.

“Mamo,” he said, tugging. “Is that the street where they lived?”

He pointed, as he always pointed, at the wall.

The wall stood three streets over, grey and rough and three metres high, running through the city like a scar that had never been allowed to close. Behind it, they said, the buildings were empty now. They said there were no more Jews in Warsaw. They said a great deal on the official posters, which were glued to every lamppost and kiosk and boarded-up shopfront in the General Government, and Marianna had learned to read between every article. She read between the lines of the official newspaper too, though she no longer bought it. She read between the lines of everything. Between the lines was the only country left to live in.

“No,” she said. “Eat your bread.”

He ate the heel she had saved him. She looked at the wall anyway. Everyone did, although people with sense did it quickly, without pausing, because the guards on the wall shot without warning and a long look was suspicious. She caught herself looking a moment too long and made herself study the cracked pavement instead.

It was June, deep June, and the linden tree across from the baker's had come into blossom early, furious with it, reckless, covering the pavement in small pale-green stars. The smell reached her where she stood: sweet, heavy, too generous. She had forgotten the smell of June. The war had taught her to forget it on purpose, because remembering it hurt in a way that ration-card hunger never did.

“Mamo.” Alicja lowered the book. “The cart.”

There was a lorry at the corner. Not milk. Soldiers.

She did not watch. She had learned, in the way you learn anything that keeps you alive, how not to watch. How to be a grey woman with a shopping bag. How to be furniture. She gave the children her eyes to keep them busy — *look at me, look at me only* — and the lorry passed, and when the real cart finally came along, horse-drawn, an old man asleep over the reins, the queue shuffled forward, and Marianna got her two scoops of thin, blue milk into the tin pot she had carried since before the war, since her wedding, since everything, and turned for home.

* * *

The streets were having one of their terrible Thursdays. Thursday was market day in the old city, and these days the military authorities had taken to cordoning streets near the wall for “traffic and security measures,” which meant the mornings belonged to lorries and guards and sudden shouting, and the afternoons belonged to the smell of lime and ash that never quite left the air.

They took the long way, past Plac Trzech Krzyży, which was safe enough if you didn't look at the posters. Alicja walked two steps ahead, hands in pockets, being almost sixteen and above everything. Wojtek trailed with the string of his tin cup clutched in his fist.

On Żelazna Street, near the junction, there was a cordon.

Soldiers. More than usual. A staff car, dark green, polished, military plates. Two lorries. And beyond the cordon, a column of men moving under escort, filing in the direction of the station. The men were not in uniform. They carried bundles. One of them limped. One of them stopped and looked at nothing, at the sky, in the way a person looks when he is memorizing something for the last time. One of them was very young, perhaps Alicja's age, and he looked at Alicja, through Alicja, as though seeing the girl he might have been if the world had not arranged itself this way.

Marianna turned Wojtek bodily away. “Not this street.”

But Wojtek had seen the lorries, and the rifles, and the column, and something in him — the age, the not-yet — wanted to know the name of it. He turned his head too.

“Mamo, the bread card—”

Her hand went to her bag by reflex.

The card was not in it.

She had put it in the pocket of her coat, she was almost sure, and in the crush of the queue the pocket had torn — she saw it now, the seam gaping at her hip — and the card itself, wrapped in its wax paper with the rationing stamps flashing through the tear, had fallen to the gutter.

It lay at the very edge of the cordon. On the clean side as far as the soldiers were concerned, but the gutter didn't care about lines drawn on a map.

Wojtek did not hesitate. He was nine. He went after his bread card.

She caught his collar, but the collar was small and he was quick, and the string of his tin cup caught on a nail in the wall, and by the time she reached him he had the card, but he had also put himself, his small self, directly in the path of the cordon.

A soldier shouted.

Then the world came apart.

* * *

“Stehenbleiben!” the guard bellowed, and in the same instant another soldier had Wojtek by the arm, not gently, not gently at all, and Alicja was back in a single bound, screaming her brother's name in a raw voice that didn't sound like Alicja's voice at all, and Marianna was shouting — what, she didn't know later, only that it was Polish, or German, or a word that belonged to neither language — reaching, pulling at the soldier's sleeve, her ration card forgotten, her dignity forgotten, everything forgotten except the child inside the uniform's grip.

The guard shouted again and swung the rifle butt toward her hand to knock her away.

And then a car door opened. The staff car. And a man got out.

He was tall. He wore the grey-green of the Wehrmacht with the collar tabs of a staff Major, the visor cap of an officer, boots polished to a black, impossible shine — cleaner than the car, cleaner than the street, cleaner than anything that had a right to exist in this city in 1943. He was young for the rank. Not young — not twenty-three, not anymore — but the kind of face that had kept its shape. A long face. A cleft in the chin. Grey at the temples that should not have suited him and did.

He looked at the soldier. He said one short sentence in German. The soldier released Wojtek instantly, stepped back, and his face went a colour Marianna would remember later.

Then the officer looked at her.

The street went quiet for her. The lorries, the soldiers, the column of men shuffling away — all of it went away the way sound goes under water. There was only the grey-green uniform, the staff cap, and the face under the visor that she had last seen almost twenty years ago, wet on a platform in Danzig, waving.

Konrad Brandt.

He was older. He had aged into the face he had been going to have all along. His eyes were the same grey-blue. His mouth was the same mouth. There was a line between his brows that had not been there in 1924, and it would have broken some other woman's heart, that line, because she knew exactly what the years had filed into it.

She did not speak.

He looked at her, and she saw it happen behind his eyes: the whole architecture of a memory coming down — the bookshop, the linden, the platform, the telegram, everything — falling in order, the way buildings fall in the films: floor by floor, on the inside, before the outside moves.

“Marianna Frontczak,” he said.

It was not a question. His voice was lower, German-polished, careful.

She stood in the gutter in her thin summer coat with her son weeping against her hip, with her daughter gripping her sleeve, with the soldiers frozen around them and the prisoners gone and the staff car shining, and she heard herself answer:

“No,” she said. “I am nobody. You are mistaken.”

The Major held her eyes for one long moment. Whatever he saw there, he accepted it. He turned to the guard, said two more words in German, and then, without looking at her again, got back into the car. It pulled away clean and dark, and the cordon snapped back into place, and the soldiers moved the column along.

She found she was holding Wojtek by both shoulders.

“Home,” she said. Nobody argued with that voice, not even Alicja.

They walked home without speaking. The lindens dropped blossom on them twice.

* * *

Warsaw in June of 1924 was all dust and horse chestnuts and the trams running with flowers on the roof. Marianna Tarnowska was twenty-three years old and she worked in her father's bookshop, one shopfront off Krakowskie Przedmieście, and she had never met a German she was permitted to like.

There was no hatred in it then, or little enough. Her father had not fought; her mother had kept her head down through every occupation, every rising, every century. The Germans, the Russians, the Austrians — to her mother they were weather. You dressed for what came. You survived. You did not love the weather.

But Konrad Brandt was not weather. He was a person. He came into the shop in the second week of May to ask, in halting, beautiful Polish, for technical drawings of a waterworks at Tczew, and her father was busy in the back with the binding, and Marianna, who had read every book she hadn't sold, sold him the text that explained it instead. He paid in good silver. He came again the following Saturday to say the drawings were out of print. He came again the week after that to say the bookshop was the only one in Warsaw that kept the edition. He came again. He kept a face so earnest about it that even her father saw it and said nothing.

He was an engineer, fresh from Danzig — the Free City, as everyone called it, though he called it home — come to study hydraulics at the Warsaw Polytechnic. He spoke Polish with a German accent, and then with less and less of one, and then with none at all, and by June he was coming to the shop every Saturday, and then every day; by late June he was there before the shutters went up; and it was understood without anyone saying it that he was coming because of her.

They walked in the park by Agrykola. Once they went to Wilanów Palace on a Sunday with a crowd of students and ate cold sausage out of a newspaper. Once they stood on the bridge over the Vistula watching the barges, and he told her he loved her in both languages — in German first, and then, deliberately, in Polish, because he said German was the language he thought in but Polish was the language he felt in, and she was the one thing in either country he had never had to think about.

“You are the only thing in Poland or Germany I trust,” he said. He was half laughing, and she struck him with the rolled newspaper she was holding and told him to never say such a pompous thing again.

He kissed her under the linden trees at Krakowskie Przedmieście in the last week of June, 1924. The trees were in bloom. The smell of them hung in the air like honey and sunlight. She had a handkerchief crushed in her fist and her heart was beating somewhere above her shoulders and she was not thinking about Danzig or politics or her mother's raised eyebrow, and he said something in German she didn't understand and then did not translate, smiling, because that untranslatable thing was for him alone now, she understood, and she accepted that as fair.

They wrote to each other after he left for the station. The first letter came six days after he did — he had written it on the train and posted it from Poznań. She read it at the counter between customers, and then she read it again, and then she put it in the tea tin with the registration papers, where the marriage documents would later join it. She told her mother that her friend from the Polytechnic was staying with her aunt in Danzig. Her mother, who saw everything and commented on nothing, nodded once and went on shelling peas.

There was a café on Mokotowska where students from the Polytechnic used to gather, with marble tables and a parrot in a cage nobody had claimed since 1919. They had gone there the evening before he left for the station. He had a gram negative talk then, she remembered, about asking for her hand properly, about the Free City being a bridge, about the shipyard his father was failing to leave him — too heavy a subject for the closing hour of June — and he had stopped, and drunk his black coffee, and said the simplest thing of all: “When it is settled, I will come back and ask.” She had believed him. There was never a reason to doubt him. There was only the world, which had reasons of its own.

His father died in September, 1924, in Danzig. He got the telegram at the Polytechnic, in the middle of a lecture; she was with him — she had brought him his coffee, as she always brought it — and she held his hand the whole way across the city to the Western Station, and she held it on the platform too, when the time came. It was October, already cold, the lindens bare, the cobbles wet with early rain.

He wrote. She kept every letter. Four letters in October; three in November; two in December. The news was always the same: the funeral, the shipyard accounts, the debts, the business his sister would not help him settle, the creditors who would not stop. *When it is settled, I will come back.* *Wait for me.* *Every day I think of you.* *Don't listen to the newspapers about the Free City — nothing there concerns us.* She wrote back twice in December. He wrote again in January.

Then there were no letters.

She wrote in February, and again in March of 1925, and in April her father had a heart attack and died in the bookshop, under the counter she had polished every morning since she was a girl, and her mother was ill, and the shop was failing, and there was no money for stamps to Danzig. She married Jozef Frontczak in September because Jozef was steady and kind and loved her children when they came — Alicja first, then Wojtek. Jozef the railway clerk, with his cap and his polite hands and his quiet Sunday walks, who never once suspected what the past had been, and who loved her truly, in his way, the way a man loves a house he has been permitted to live in.

The bookshop burned in the siege of 1939. She saved exactly two things from the fire: the ration books and the wedding band, and the book with his inscription in it, the hydraulics monograph with *Dla Marianny, pod lipą, zawsze* on the flyleaf. There was no reason to save the book. There was never a reason. That was why she saved it.

She had not seen Konrad Brandt in twenty years. She had not heard his name. She had told no one. Not Jozef, not her mother before she died, not her sister, not her priest. She had kept him the way you keep a bruise — private, and aching, and finally numb.

He was a Major now, in a city he had once claimed to trust her above.

* * *

For the rest of the day, everything was wrong.

Wojtek couldn't stop talking about it at the table: the soldiers, the shouting, the car, *the officer*. Alicja said nothing, but she watched her mother across the soup pot, and her eyes were the eyes of a girl who had been reading between lines since she was eleven.

Marianna served the soup. Barley. No meat, and what fat it had seen was pork fat from a friend of a friend, smoked hard as horn. She said: “Eat,” and “Wash your hands,” and “Don't go out after dark,” and when Wojtek asked — because of course he asked — who the officer was, she said: “Someone your father would have driven politely around.”

That night she slept badly. She dreamed of the wall, and the wall had the bookshop inside it, and the linden was blooming on top of the ruins, and every blossom was a letter that had never arrived.

Three days later, a notice came.

*Notice of Commandeering of Lodging and Damage Assessment.*

The entire building — their building, the tenement on Ceglana Street, third floor, four rooms, shared staircase, one broken window left unmended since Jozef — was subject to inspection and possible commandeering by the Wehrmacht for the purposes of billeting. Residents were required to report, with papers and keys, to the Housing Office on Praga, on Tuesday, 13 July, at 0900.

Date of issue: 9 July 1943.

Marianna read the notice three times. Then she put it flat on the table and sat down and looked at it, and Alicja read it over her shoulder and went and put the kettle on, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't dangerous.

“Write to my aunt in Kraków,” Marianna said at last, to Alicja. “Ask her if she still has the feather bed.”

Alicja looked at her mother for a long moment.

“Yes, Mamo,” she said.

* * *

Tuesday. The Housing Office. The queue outside the door was mostly women. The smell of the office was dust, disinfectant, leather, and coal smoke. Through the high windows came the sound of trams, and, distantly, the artillery everyone in the city had learned not to hear.

She waited two hours. When her turn came, she was shown into a room with filing cabinets, a map of the district on the wall, a portrait of the Führer above the map — she averted her eyes from it with the expert ease of the occupied — and a desk, and behind the desk, standing with his back to her, inspecting requisition forms with a ruler, was a man in uniform.

The door closed behind her.

For a moment he didn't turn. The ruler moved down the paper with small, precise taps; the sound of it was very loud. Then he put the ruler down and turned.

He was in the same uniform, only here it didn't shine. Here it was simply grey-green, the colour of everything in this city now.

“Mrs. Frontczak,” he said.

He didn't say *Marianna*. He used the formal German honorific for a married woman, and it was like a door opening onto the abyss.

“Please sit.”

She sat. He sat as well, perched on the edge of the desk — which was somehow worse than a chair on the other side of it, worse than a desk between them, as though the desk had been the last barrier and he had chosen, badly, to dismantle it. Up close, the lines in his face were deeper than they had been in the street. There was a scar at his temple, fine as a hair, that hadn't been there when he was twenty-two.

“Your building,” he said, in German. Then, in Polish, rough with long disuse: “The Ceglana tenement. Your papers.”

She put the file on the desk. The meldekarte. Jozef's death certificate. Alicja's birth certificate. Wojtek's. The building card. The registration of the apartment, the key inventory, the inspection notice, the sewing-machine permit. Everything she owned on paper, in a folder, in a small life.

He didn't look at the papers. He looked at her.

“It was not you I wanted to see,” he said. “But it is you they sent me.”

The room was very still. Somewhere in the building a typewriter rattled; somewhere distant, a door banged.

“You knew,” she said. Her voice came out steady. She was surprised by it. “You knew my building was on this list.”

“I knew,” he said. “When the requisition lists crossed my desk, I saw the name. The street. The number. I—” He stopped. Started again. “I asked for Warsaw. When they offered me the posting, they asked where I wanted to go. I asked for Warsaw.”

“Why?”

He didn't answer at once. He went to the window instead, and looked out at the street, and for a second she saw him as he had been, standing at the window of the bookshop, not wanting to leave, saying *stay*.

“I have wondered that myself every day for two years,” he said. “I had a hope. I don't know what it was. I don't know that I ever let myself know. I have been an officer in this army for nine years. I sign paper. I move paper. I have requisitioned food for districts you have only heard of. I have never—” He turned. “I have never shot anyone, Mrs. Frontczak. I want you to know that. I don't say it because it makes me innocent. It doesn't. I move paper. Paper is never innocent.”

He came back to the desk.

“But I am not here to defend myself. I am here to say one thing, and then I will do my duty, and you will think of me what you must.”

He opened the drawer of the desk, and took out a single sheet of paper, and put it in front of her. A requisition form. Stamped. In the box marked *Building and occupancy*, someone had written, in a precise, German hand: *STORNO — exempt from requisition — administrative correction — by order of the Housing Officer.*

Below the stamp was a signature she could not read, and a date: 11 July 1943, two days before this meeting had been scheduled.

“It is cancelled,” he said. “The building is exempt. No one will billet here.”

She stared at the paper. She put her hands in her lap because they had started to shake.

“I don't want your mercy,” she said.

“No,” he said. “I know. But it is not mercy. It is—”

“What.”

“A debt,” he said. “Twenty years old. I never paid it. I have been trying to find the address for a long time.”

She stood. Her chair made a small sound on the floorboards.

“Don't,” she said. “Don't say *address* to me. I was never—”

“You were. You were the address I carried out of every requisition office and every train station and every border crossing for twenty years. You still are.”

“Don't.”

“Marianna.” He said it as he had said it in 1924, in Polish, without ceremony. *Marianna.* Not *Mrs. Frontczak*. Not a German officer addressing a Polish widow. Just his voice, the voice from before. “I came back in November. November of 1924. I stood at the Western Station in the snow. I went to the bookshop. It was boarded up. A boy told me you'd married. In September. To Jozef— Frontczak. A railway clerk.” He touched his chest. “I had the announcement. I kept it. I kept the newspaper for a long time.”

She sat back down, because her legs no longer agreed to stand.

“But I wrote,” she said. “January. February. You never answered.”

“I never got them.”

“But you said—”

“I know what I said.” He took a long breath. “After my father died, the board of the shipyard pushed me out. My sister took me in. I was ill — typhus, the doctors said. I nearly—” He looked away. “I woke in the hospital in March of 1925. The letters had stopped. I assumed. I was proud. I was stupid. I didn't come back to Warsaw because I could not face leaving again. Later, when I tried, it came back. Return to sender. Addressed to the bookshop. The bookshop had burned.”

“The bookshop didn't burn until 1939,” she said.

He stared at her.

“The bookshop was open all of ’25 and ’26,” she said slowly. “My father died in 1925 but my mother kept it open until the siege. Your letters, if they came, would have reached us.”

The room was silent.

“Someone else would have known,” she said at last. “My mother. She knew everything. She returned the letters that mattered. Anything addressed to me in a German hand, she put in the stove. She told me once, after you left. She said: *that one is not for you.* And then, months later, she told me you had married in Danzig. A girl from Gdańsk. She had seen it in the newspaper.”

The silence became a different kind of silence.

“Your mother,” he said.

“My mother,” Marianna said. “Who loved me. Who saved me from the things that would have broken me. Who believed a German son-in-law in 1925 would have broken me worse.”

She waited for him to be angry.

He wasn't.

He was only very tired, and very old, and very much alone, and he said softly, “All these years.”

“All these years,” she agreed.

* * *

The door was closed, the windows barred, the street outside held October 1924 and every season since.

“Jozef,” she said at last. “My husband. He died in September 1939. He was a clerk at the Eastern Railway. A September bomb hit the junction. He went in to help.”

“I am sorry.”

“He was kind. He loved me. He never suspected.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“No,” he said. “I don't. But I can see the shape of the silence in you. He is why you wear your wedding band on the right hand.”

She touched her ring.

“How do you know that.”

“Because you were never a right-hand woman,” he said. “You would have given everything to hate this moment, and you can't, because you remember lindens and a bookshop and a man who brought you coffee. And because you are too honest to let your grief become a weapon.”

She wept then, for the first time since the war began, without sound, without movement. She looked at the portrait over the map, and at the lines of his face, and at the winter of 1924 and the man she had married and the war that had taken him — not with a bullet, but with twenty years of paper, and letters, and pride, and a mother with a stove.

He didn't come around the desk. He stood perfectly still until the storm passed, this officer, this enemy, this ghost, and when she was finished, she picked up her papers.

“Take the form. No one will billet. Take medicine — for the girl. Her breathing. I have access. It is nothing.”

“How did you know about Alicja?”

“You mentioned it,” he said, and looked away. “On the street. The children. I heard what you asked for.”

It occurred to her — she filed it away, for later, for the sleepless nights — that he had arranged to see her, back there at the cordon, deliberately. That was what a staff officer did: arranging. He had arranged, perhaps, everything since 11 July.

She put the form in her envelope.

“Will I see you again?” she said.

It was the question she hadn't intended to ask, and she couldn't take it back.

For a long moment he didn't answer.

“Probably not,” he said at last. “I asked for Warsaw to know whether you were alive, and to close an account twenty years overdue. I have nothing more to give that this city doesn't already take.” He straightened his collar. “Don't come here again. If you need help — a card, a permit, anything — the housing office keeps a file clerk, a Polish woman, Mrs. Kopec. Leave a note with her. Do not write my name.”

She was at the door when he said, “Marianna.”

She stopped.

“The linden tree on Ceglana. By the post office. It is still standing. I checked.”

He didn't say *take care of it*. He didn't say *remember*. He didn't say *goodbye*.

He simply said the name of the tree.

* * *

That night, after the children were asleep, Marianna took the book from the bottom of the wardrobe, the hydraulics monograph with its black cloth cover and its faint smell of another house. The inscription was still there on the flyleaf: *Dla Marianny, pod lipą, zawsze.* For Marianna. Under the linden, always. She sat with it for a long time. The war had reduced the world to bread and keys and silence, but it could not reduce the world to that. She thought about the mother she had never thanked, the husband she had never fooled, the man she had never forgiven, and the daughter who read between the lines. Somewhere, she knew, between the stove and the inscription, between the September bomb and the Tuesday notice, between the tree by the post office and the tin cup full of blossoms, there was a woman who had survived everything by keeping two things out of the fire. She put the book back. She slept, finally, and dreamed of nothing, or of June — she couldn't tell the difference anymore, and had stopped trying.

* * *

Four days later, on Sunday, they walked to the post office on Ceglana Street as though it were any Sunday, as though there were no war and no wall and no officer with a silver-pipped collar and twenty years of letters burned in a stove.

They passed the square where the Great Synagogue had stood until the spring; it was rubble now, fenced with military barriers, dust in the sunlight, once among the largest synagogues in the world and no longer there at all. Wojtek asked what it had been, and Marianna said, “A church,” because at nine, that was an answer he could hold.

Wojtek ran ahead. Alicja walked beside her mother, hands in pockets, waiting.

The tree was there. An old tree, nearly as old as the street, with a trunk like a barrel and branches that reached over the post office roof and the telegraph wires. It had dropped its blossoms a week early — the heat, the artillery dust — but the blossoms lay thick on the pavement, papery and pale gold, and the smell of them hung in the warm air like something remembered from a dream you had almost lost.

Marianna stopped at the foot of it.

“Mamo?” Alicja said.

“You'll understand someday,” her mother said. “But not today. Not for a long time.”

Alicja looked at the tree, and at the blossoms, and at her mother's face, and something settled in her that would perhaps stay for the rest of her life — the knowledge that her mother had been twenty-three once, and had loved something she could not have, and had carried it anyway, into widowhood and war and everything after.

“All right,” Alicja said.

Wojtek came back breathless, one tin cup full of blossoms.

“For you, Mamo,” he said.

She took the cup. She smelled the flowers. The artillery was very far away, and the city was not empty of them, however much they wrote on the posters, and somewhere across the river, or behind the wall, or in a railway yard, a man was signing paper, and his paper moved and hers did not billet, and somewhere a mother had fed letters to a stove, and somewhere a young man in a hospital in Danzig had slept through the spring of 1925 believing he was unloved.

A parcel had come that morning, she hadn't told the children: real coffee, in a tin, with a stamp of the German army's quartermaster stores, and a small paper twist of white tablets marked for Alicja's breathing, and no note, and no name, and never a name. It lay at the bottom of her wardrobe now, under the spare buttons and the Singer manual, waiting for a winter she hoped would be mild and knew would not be.

Some things, she thought, you do not forgive.

Some things, you do not forget.

The rest — the small impossible things, the linden, the light, the blossoms in a tin cup — you carry, because you have children, and they have to know that before all of this, there was June.

“Come on,” she said softly. “We'll be late for church.”

They walked home through the blossoms, the three of them, and did not look back.

*No tree, no bread card, and no story survives this city. But some things, against all odds, were carried out.*
