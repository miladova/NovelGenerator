# The Unmaking of a Name

* A Marauders-era one-shot — Sirius Black’s sister / James Potter

---

By the time the first snow settled on the Hogwarts grounds, Mara Black had learned there were two kinds of silence.

There was the good sort: the hush of the library after dinner, the breathless pause before a Snitch broke left, the warm quiet that came when Sirius fell asleep in the common room with his head tipped against her shoulder. And there was the other sort. The kind that lived in the Black family drawing room, where portraits watched you decide whether to speak and your mother could make a whole sentence out of one lifted eyebrow.

Mara had not heard that second silence in months.

It was waiting for her in the envelope on her breakfast plate.

The parchment was thick, cream-colored, and sealed with the Black crest. It had appeared beside her pumpkin juice sometime between the toast and James Potter’s attempt to charm a sausage into singing the school song. The envelope was addressed in her mother’s narrow, slanted hand.

Mara stared at it. The Great Hall went on around her. Cutlery clinked; owls swept between the tables; someone at the Hufflepuff end cheered as a tiny flock of paper birds burst from a cereal bowl.

Beside her, Sirius stopped laughing.

“Don’t,” he said.

“I haven’t done anything.”

“You’re looking at it like you’re about to set it on fire.”

“I might.”

“Do it after I’ve finished breakfast.” He reached for the envelope, then thought better of it. “Actually, don’t. It’ll probably have some sort of family curse on it. Mother does love a dramatic entrance.”

Across the table, James lowered the newspaper he had been pretending to read. “If it’s cursed, I know a very reliable counter-curse.”

“You know a very reliable way to make things explode,” said Remus.

“Same thing, if you’re optimistic.”

Mara put the letter in her pocket.

Sirius’s jaw tightened. He knew that look, too. He had seen it on her face the night she’d packed a trunk in the middle of August and told him she wasn’t going back to Grimmauld Place. He had offered to come with her. She had told him he couldn’t spend his whole life being her shield. He had said, “Watch me,” and she had made him promise not to follow.

He had kept the promise. Mostly.

“You don’t have to open it,” he said now.

“I know.”

“You don’t have to go home over Christmas.”

“I know that, too.”

“Good.” He picked up his fork. “Then we’re all agreed. It’s a stupid letter, and we’re having a normal day.”

“Your plan for normality is usually to set something on fire,” Mara said.

“Exactly. Familiar ground.”

James grinned at her over the rim of his goblet. “If we’re starting a fire, I vote the Slytherin common room. Purely for the aesthetic.”

“You’re not starting any fires,” Remus said.

“Fine. A tasteful bit of smoke.”

Mara stood before the letter could grow heavier in her pocket. “I have Arithmancy.”

“You have breakfast,” Sirius said, watching her untouched plate.

“I’m full.”

“Of what?”

“Disappointment.” She leaned down and kissed his hair before he could answer. It was an old, childish gesture, one he pretended to hate and never moved away from. Then she crossed the Great Hall with the envelope pressing against her ribs.

She waited until the empty classroom on the third floor to open it.

> Mara,
>
> Your absence has become an embarrassment. You will return to Grimmauld Place on the twenty-first. Your father and I expect you to present yourself properly at the Yule gathering. Mr. Rosier will be in attendance, as will his family. You are old enough to understand the importance of making a sensible match and putting this childish rebellion aside.
>
> The family name is not a toy to be discarded when it becomes inconvenient. You will remember who you are.
>
> Walburga Black

Mara read it once. Then again, though the words did not change.

Her mother had not written since the Sorting. Not when Sirius left. Not when Mara was placed in Gryffindor, red and gold on her shoulders like a declaration of war. Not when she’d stopped coming home for summers. Now, at seventeen, she was expected to return and smile at a boy whose father called Muggle-borns a stain on the world.

A sensible match.

She folded the letter into smaller and smaller squares until the paper softened at the creases. Her hands were steady. That was the thing about fear: it did not always shake you. Sometimes it made you very, very still.

A chair scraped in the corridor. Mara drew her wand.

“Don’t hex me,” James called through the open door. “I’ve already had a rough morning.”

“What did you do?”

“Nothing you can prove.” He leaned into the doorway, glasses crooked, tie loose. “I was sent to find you. Sirius says you’ve been missing long enough that he’s gone from pretending not to worry to actively worrying.”

“He sent you?”

“He said, and I quote, ‘Go bother her until she starts looking annoyed. Then she’ll be all right.’”

“That sounds like Sirius.”

“I thought so.” James came in but didn’t sit. He had learned, somehow, that Mara hated being cornered. “You don’t have to tell me what it says.”

“Good.”

“Although I can guess. Is it an invitation to join the family’s annual Most Oppressive Christmas party?”

She looked at him. “Something like that.”

“Do you want me to hex Rosier?”

“No.”

“Right. Because you’re perfectly capable of hexing Rosier yourself.”

“I don’t want to hex anyone.”

“Then I’ll stand nearby and look threatening.”

“You’re terrible at looking threatening.”

“I am devastatingly handsome. People confuse the two.”

A laugh escaped her before she could stop it. James smiled, relieved by the sound, and that was worse than if he’d asked what the letter said. He wanted her to be all right. He wanted to fix it. James Potter had always believed most problems could be solved with a well-aimed spell, a faster broom, or enough audacity to make the universe give in.

Mara slipped the letter into her bag. “I’m fine.”

“Could you try saying that in a way I might believe?”

“I’m not fine. But I will be.”

“Better.” He held out his hand, palm up. “Come on. I’ve been sent to escort you to Arithmancy. I intend to do it brilliantly.”

“I’m not going to Arithmancy.”

“Then I’ll escort you wherever you are going.”

“I’m going to the library.”

“Excellent. I love the library.”

“You hate the library.”

“I love you in the library.”

The words landed between them, dressed up as a joke. James’s grin stayed fixed, but the tips of his ears went pink. Mara looked down at the offered hand. She could take it; she could pretend she hadn’t heard the sentence underneath the sentence. That was what they did. They spent years walking the edge of something and calling it friendship.

She took his hand.

“Try not to get us thrown out,” she said.

“I make no promises.”

---

The week before Christmas arrived in a flurry of snow and unfinished essays. Hogwarts put garlands around the banisters, enchanted frost on the windows, and a dozen tiny singing trees in the entrance hall. The castle was trying very hard to be cheerful.

Mara was trying very hard to be ordinary.

She went to class. She studied. She helped Sirius and James “test” a new dungbomb that turned out to release a cloud of blue butterflies and a smell like burnt treacle. She laughed when Peter got one caught in his hair. She listened to Remus explain, with the weary patience of a prefect, that no, they could not set the butterflies loose in Filch’s office.

At night she lay awake thinking of the Yule gathering.

She had not told Sirius the details. She had told him her mother wanted her home, and he had gone so quiet that she wished she had lied. Sirius knew what it meant to be made into a symbol by their family. He had spent years clawing himself free of it, and the thought that Mara might be dragged back into that house made a kind of helpless fury move behind his eyes.

On the evening of the last Hogsmeade weekend, he found her in the courtyard, perched on a stone ledge beneath a bare tree. The sky was the color of old pewter. Snow freckled the shoulders of her coat.

“You’re freezing,” he said.

“I’m thinking.”

“Do it somewhere warm.”

“I’m thinking about going.”

His expression sharpened. “Home?”

“To the gathering. If I don’t, they’ll say I’m frightened. Or ungrateful. Or that you’ve poisoned me against them.”

“I have poisoned you against them. It’s one of my better qualities.”

“Sirius.”

He shoved his hands into his pockets. There was a silver ring on one of his fingers, a family heirloom he’d never worn until he left home. Now he wore it as a joke, he said. Mara suspected it was something else: proof that the Black name didn’t own every object it touched.

“They don’t get to decide your life,” he said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

She let the question sit. “I don’t want to spend the rest of my life being the reason you hate them.”

“I don’t need a reason.”

“That’s not what I mean.” She pushed a flake of snow from her sleeve. “You left. You chose. I stayed because I thought I could keep things from getting worse for you. I thought if I was good enough, quiet enough, they might stop punishing you for being yourself.”

Sirius’s mouth went crooked with pain. “You were a kid.”

“So were you.”

“Yeah, and I was a spectacularly stupid kid.”

“You’re still spectacularly stupid.”

“Good. I’d hate to lose my consistency.” He sat beside her, shoulder to shoulder. “You don’t owe me a thing. Not staying. Not leaving. Not hating them. If you go to that party, it’s because you want to. If you don’t, it’s because you don’t. And if you decide you need to do something loud enough to make Mother’s portraits faint, I’ll help you pick the spell.”

Mara leaned against him. The two of them sat until the cold crept through their robes.

A door opened behind them. James came out carrying two mugs and a wool blanket. He took one look at the siblings and did not make a joke.

“Hot chocolate,” he said, offering a mug to Mara. “And a blanket. I’d bring a warming charm, but I’ve been told that’s ‘not how you use warming charms,’ which seems like a suspiciously narrow interpretation.”

“Who told you that?” Mara asked.

“Remus.”

“Remus is right.”

“He’s right in a very smug way.” James draped the blanket over both of them. It was too small to cover their legs, and he pretended not to notice when Mara’s shoulder brushed his. “I can bring another.”

“Stay,” Sirius said.

James stayed. He perched on the ledge on Mara’s other side, his own mug steaming in the cold. For a while, the three of them watched snow settle on the courtyard stones. Sirius told a story about the time he’d swapped the labels on all the Black family portraits. James had heard it before but laughed at the right parts. Mara let the warmth of the mug seep into her fingers.

When the tower clock struck nine, Sirius stood. “I have to meet Remus. He’s pretending he doesn’t need help with the patrol schedule.”

“He doesn’t,” James said.

“He absolutely does.” Sirius kissed Mara’s forehead. “Don’t go anywhere cold.”

“Bossy.”

“Always.” He started toward the door, then glanced at James. The look he gave him was a warning dressed as a brotherly smile. James nodded once, as if he understood the whole language of it.

When Sirius was gone, the courtyard seemed larger.

James nudged a loose stone with his boot. “He worries.”

“He always has.”

“You worry about him, too.”

“Of course.”

“Do you ever worry about yourself?”

Mara looked at him. Snow had caught in his hair, melting into dark curls. “I try not to.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s easier to be brave when I pretend it’s for someone else.”

James’s face changed. She had seen him angry, triumphant, laughing until he cried. This was quieter. “You don’t have to prove anything to me.”

“I know.”

“I mean it. You don’t have to come home with me, or run away, or fight your mother, or—”

“James.”

“Sorry.” He swallowed. “I just hate that she can make you feel like you have to earn a place in the world.”

Mara watched his breath cloud the air. “You don’t have to earn a place in the world?”

“I’m a Potter. We’re famously insufferable about having a place everywhere.”

“Must be nice.”

“It is.” His smile was faint. “But I think you’ve got more of a place than you know. Here. With Sirius. With us.”

“With you?”

He looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment there was no grin to hide behind. “Especially with me.”

Mara’s heart moved against her ribs, startled awake. She wanted to ask him what he meant. She wanted to pretend she didn’t know. She wanted to say something that would make it safe for both of them.

Instead, she stood and handed him her empty mug. “I should go inside.”

James took it. “All right.”

They walked back to the castle with a few inches of space between them and everything they hadn’t said moving in that narrow strip of air.

---

The night before the train home, Mara found the second letter.

It had been slipped under the door of the Gryffindor common room, though no one admitted seeing it arrive. This one was shorter, written in a hand she didn’t know.

> Miss Black,
>
> My son assures me that your family has agreed to a formal understanding. It would be a shame if your absence were taken for discourtesy. We shall expect you at the gathering.
>
> Abraxas Malfoy

She read it by the fire. The common room was nearly empty; most of the younger students had gone to bed, and Sirius was upstairs packing. James sat on the rug with a stack of parchment, supposedly working on an essay. He had been staring at the same sentence for ten minutes.

Mara folded the letter.

“Who’s it from?” he asked.

“No one.”

“That’s a name on the bottom.”

“It’s nothing.”

“Mara.”

She looked up. James had set his quill down. “I’m not asking because I think you owe me an explanation,” he said. “I’m asking because you look like you’re about to disappear.”

“I have to go home.”

“Do you?”

“I can’t let them decide I’m afraid.”

“That’s not the same as wanting to go.”

“No.” Her fingers tightened around the parchment. “It isn’t.”

James rose and sat on the sofa opposite. “Then don’t.”

“It’s not that simple.”

“I know.”

“You don’t. Your parents love you. They don’t send you letters telling you who you’re supposed to marry.”

“No,” he said softly. “They don’t.”

The agreement in his voice made her feel worse, not better. He was not trying to claim he understood. He was trying to listen. Mara wished he would make a joke, so she could be annoyed and safe again.

“My mother thinks this is already settled,” she said. “Apparently she and the Malfoys have been discussing it. I’m meant to appear at a party, smile at Rosier, and let the adults pretend I’ve agreed to a life I never chose.”

“Then don’t let them.”

“They’ll say I’m dishonoring the family.”

“You are.”

Her chin lifted.

“Not because you’re wrong,” James added. “Because they’ve made the family into something that deserves dishonor.”

“You make it sound easy.”

“No. I make it sound necessary.” He leaned forward, forearms on his knees. “There’s a difference.”

Mara stared at the crackling fire. “Sirius can’t keep saving me.”

“He isn’t saving you. He’s your brother.”

“He left because of me.”

“He left because your family was cruel. You were the reason he stayed as long as he did, not the reason he went.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?”

She hated that he and Sirius had chosen the same question. She hated more that she had no answer.

James continued, careful now. “You don’t have to go back to prove you’re not scared. And you don’t have to stay away to prove you’re free. You can make the choice that gets you through tomorrow. Then you can make another one the day after that.”

She looked at him. “When did you get wise?”

“Don’t tell anyone. I have a reputation.”

“Your reputation is mostly noise.”

“Then my secret’s safe.” His eyes held hers. “If you don’t want to be alone over Christmas, my parents said there’s room at home. Sirius is coming. Remus too, if he can escape his aunt’s questions about his studies. Mum has already planned to feed us enough to last until Easter.”

“I can’t impose on your family.”

“You wouldn’t be imposing.”

“You’re sure?”

“My mum would be offended if I suggested otherwise.”

Mara could picture it too easily: warm windows, a kitchen crowded with people, James’s mother pressing another helping onto Sirius’s plate and pretending not to notice when he went quiet at the sound of a holiday song. She could picture a place where no one asked her to wear a crest.

“Would you want me there?” she asked.

The question escaped before she could soften it.

James went very still. “Yes.”

“Because Sirius wants me there?”

“Because I want you there.”

He said it without a grin, without an escape hatch. Mara felt the words settle into the room.

“James—”

“I know. I’m not asking for anything. I just thought you should know.”

“What if I don’t know what I want?”

“Then you have time to find out.”

She looked at the letter in her hand. “My mother will send another.”

“Let her.”

“She’ll keep sending them.”

“Then we’ll keep not answering.”

“We?”

“Unless you’d rather do it alone.”

Mara set the letter on the table. It seemed smaller now. Still ugly. Still real. But not an order carved into stone.

“I’ll go to the station tomorrow,” she said. “I’ll get on the train. And I’ll go to your house.”

James nodded, like he would not celebrate until she was sure. “All right.”

“And I’m not going because you asked.”

“All right.”

“I’m going because I want to.”

His smile came slowly. “That’s even better.”

“Don’t make it sentimental.”

“I wouldn’t dream of it.”

She stood. “I’m going to bed.”

“Good idea.”

At the foot of the stairs, Mara turned. “James?”

“Yeah?”

“Thank you for not trying to hex anyone.”

“I can still do it tomorrow.”

“Good night.”

“Good night, Mara.”

She climbed the stairs before her face could betray how much she wanted to kiss him.

---

On the morning of the twenty-first, Hogwarts was all trunks and scarves and shouted farewells. The train hissed beyond the station windows. Snow whipped along the platform, catching in the seams of Mara’s gloves.

Sirius stood beside her, a trunk at his heel and a careless expression arranged over the tension in his shoulders. James was arguing with Peter about whether a cauldron counted as luggage if it was being carried by hand. Remus had a book open and was somehow still listening to everything.

Mara had brought one trunk. It held everything she needed and very little she owned.

“You could still come to mine,” Sirius said. “We could spend the holiday setting the Black family tree on fire.”

“I thought you were going to your parents’.”

“I am. Dad says he’s got a surprise for me.”

“Your dad’s idea of a surprise is a new broom polish.”

“He can be unpredictable.”

“You could come with me,” James said, appearing at her other shoulder. “Mum’s made enough food for a small army.”

“Your family isn’t a small army.”

“Dad counts as at least six people when he’s hungry.”

“Good to know.”

The whistle shrieked. Students surged for the train.

Mara’s palm began to sweat inside her glove. All the way to the station, she had been telling herself the decision was easy. One train, one platform, one destination. But across the tracks, behind the steam and bodies, she could almost see the road to Grimmauld Place. The house was not here. Her mother was not here. Still, the old rules had a way of following her.

Sirius squeezed her shoulder. “You don’t have to make it pretty.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to explain it to me.”

“I know.”

“And you don’t have to take care of me.”

She turned to him. “I know.”

His smile was strained but real. “Good.”

Mara looked at the train. Then at James, who was trying very hard not to look as though her answer mattered to him. His hand rested on the handle of her trunk. He did not reach for her. He waited.

“Potter house,” she said.

James’s shoulders loosened. “Potter house.”

“Don’t look so pleased. I’m only coming for the food.”

“Obviously. No other reason.”

“Or the fire.”

“We have several.”

Sirius leaned in and kissed the top of her head. “You’ll write?”

“Every day.”

“You’ll write once.”

“Every other day.”

“Twice.”

“Fine. Twice.”

He held her for a moment, fierce and brief. “I love you, Mara.”

“I love you, too.”

He let go first. James took the trunk, and they climbed onto the train together.

They found a compartment with Remus and Peter. Outside, the platform drifted past in a blur of smoke and color. Sirius waved through the glass until the train turned, and Mara waved back until he disappeared.

The compartment settled into the familiar rhythm of their friends: Peter unpacking sweets, Remus correcting the position of a chess piece, James taking up too much space and acting as if he had not. Mara sat by the window, her shoulder against the glass.

The folded letter was still in her pocket. She pulled it out and looked at the Black seal. Then she tore it cleanly in half.

James glanced over. “Does that help?”

“A little.”

“Want to tear it into smaller pieces?”

“Not yet.” She tucked the halves away. “I might need them.”

“For what?”

“To remind myself that paper can be broken.”

James’s expression softened. “That’s a good reason.”

He moved to the seat beside her. Their knees touched. Neither of them moved away.

---

The Potter house smelled like cinnamon, woodsmoke, and something baking with far too much butter. It was a low, warm place at the edge of a village, with ivy climbing the front wall and a brass knocker shaped like a lion. Lights shone in the windows. The moment they stepped inside, Euphemia Potter appeared in the hall and pulled Sirius into a hug that made him go stiff before he melted into it.

“There you are,” she said. “You’re freezing. Both of you. James, take their coats. Sirius, stop pretending you don’t like being fussed over.”

“I don’t know what you mean,” Sirius said into her shoulder.

“I’m sure.” She let him go and turned to Mara. “And you must be Mara. Welcome, darling. We’re so glad you’re here.”

The words were ordinary. Their effect was not.

Mara had been welcomed into houses before. She had been introduced, assessed, and praised for her posture. But no one had ever greeted her as if her arrival itself were a gift.

“Thank you,” she managed.

“Come in. There’s tea, and your room is ready. James, stop hovering.”

“I’m not hovering.”

“You’re standing in the middle of the hall blocking the door.”

James moved aside. “I’m making sure the door is secure.”

“Of course you are.” Euphemia ushered them toward the kitchen. “Your father’s out in the shed with a project, which means we may have to rescue the garden later. Remus is expected tomorrow, Peter’s gone home for a few days, and I’ve made enough pudding for twelve.”

“Only twelve?” Sirius asked.

“Fourteen, if James eats the way he did last year.”

“I was growing.”

“You were seventeen.”

The kitchen was bright, the table crowded with fruit and teacups. Mara stood just inside the doorway, unsure where to put her hands. James noticed and dragged out a chair for her without a word. She sat. Euphemia poured tea. Sirius began telling a story about a suit of armor that had developed a fear of stairs.

Mara listened to the warmth around her. She tried not to think about how temporary it was.

That evening, after dinner, James found her on the back step. The snow had stopped. A clear, cold sky stretched above the garden, the bare branches black against it. Inside, Euphemia and Sirius were arguing over the rules of a card game. James’s father was attempting to convince Remus, by letter, that an enchanted garden gnome was a perfectly reasonable Christmas gift.

“You vanished,” James said.

“I’m still here.”

“You know what I mean.”

She wrapped her arms around herself. “It’s strange.”

“Good strange or bad strange?”

“Both.”

He sat on the step beneath her. “Mum does that.”

“What?”

“Make people feel at home before they know if they’re allowed to.”

“Is that what she’s doing?”

“I think so.”

Mara looked through the window. Sirius was laughing, head thrown back, the firelight catching the silver of his ring. She could not remember the last time she had seen him laugh in a house without checking who might be listening.

“I thought leaving would feel like winning,” she said. “It doesn’t.”

“No.”

“It feels like I’m waiting for someone to tell me I’ve made a mistake.”

James leaned his elbows on his knees. “You might make mistakes.”

She gave him a look.

“So will I,” he said. “So will Sirius. It doesn’t mean you have to go back.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m starting to.”

He nodded. “Good.”

She watched the pale moon rise above the trees. “I used to think the Black name was a thing I had to carry carefully. Like a glass bowl. If I dropped it, everyone would blame me for the noise.”

“And now?”

“Now I think it was cracked before I ever touched it.”

James tilted his head, considering. “Then you don’t have to carry it.”

“It’s still mine.”

“Maybe. But it doesn’t get to be all of you.”

She looked at him. “You say things like that, and then you spend the next day trying to charm someone’s eyebrows green.”

“I contain multitudes.”

“You contain a great deal of trouble.”

“Also true.” He smiled, then let it fade. “Mara, I meant what I said at school. I want you here. Not because Sirius worries, not because my mum is generous, and not because I think you need saving. I want you here because when you’re in a room, I look for you first.”

Her breath caught. “James—”

“I know it’s not the perfect moment. I know there’s a lot going on. I’m not asking you to answer. I just can’t keep pretending I’m joking every time I say something true.”

The house glowed behind them. The cold made her cheeks sting. James looked suddenly younger than his confidence, waiting for her to decide what his honesty meant.

“You’ve been looking for me first for ages,” she said.

“I have.”

“I thought you liked having someone to argue with.”

“I do. You’re very good at it.”

“I thought you were like that with everyone.”

“I’m not.”

Mara remembered a thousand little things: James saving her a seat without asking; the way he always knew when her laughter was real; the afternoons he’d stayed in the library when Sirius had left for Quidditch practice, even though he hated the library. The times he had nearly said something and turned it into a joke instead.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“Because you were Sirius’s sister.”

“That’s a reason?”

“It’s a terrifying reason. If I got it wrong, I’d lose you both.”

“You can’t lose me by telling the truth.”

“I know that now.”

She stood. James looked up at her. “Do you?”

“I’m starting to.”

He rose too, careful not to close the distance. “Mara, you don’t have to—”

She kissed him.

It was not the sort of kiss that made the world disappear. The world stayed stubbornly present: the cold step beneath her feet, the warm light in the window, Sirius laughing inside. James made a surprised sound against her mouth, then lifted one hand to her cheek. He kissed her like he was trying not to presume, like he would stop if she changed her mind.

She did not change her mind.

When they parted, James rested his forehead against hers. His glasses had gone crooked.

“Was that an answer?” he whispered.

“It was a beginning.”

His smile broke open. “I can work with a beginning.”

“Don’t make me regret this.”

“I would never.”

“You absolutely would.”

“I would never make you regret kissing me.”

“That’s more believable.”

The kitchen door opened. “If you two are done making the back step colder,” Sirius called, “Mum says there’s pudding.”

Mara pulled away, laughing. James groaned. “Does he have to know everything?”

“He’s my brother. It’s his only hobby.”

Sirius stood in the doorway, arms folded. His gaze moved from Mara’s flushed face to James’s rumpled hair and crooked glasses.

“Potter,” he said.

“Sirius.”

“If you hurt her, I’ll—”

“Hex him,” Mara said. “You’ve been waiting years for an excuse.”

“I have several already.”

James held up both hands. “I’m not planning to hurt her.”

“Plans change.”

“So do people.”

Sirius stared at him. Then he looked at Mara. “You all right?”

She nodded. “I am.”

“Good.” He looked back at James. “Then I’ll only hex you a little.”

“Fair.”

Sirius went back inside. Mara started after him, but James caught her hand. Not to hold her there—just to ask.

She squeezed his fingers once.

At the table, Euphemia set a plate of pudding in front of Mara as if feeding her were the simplest answer to every difficult question. Sirius and James fell into an argument about whether a card trick counted as cheating if the cards had been bewitched. Mara listened, smiling, until the laugh in her chest stopped feeling borrowed.

Later, when the others had gone upstairs, she sat in the kitchen with a sheet of parchment and a blank page. The Black seal lay beside her, in two pieces. James hovered in the doorway until she looked up.

“Do you want company?”

“Yes.”

He took the chair opposite and let her write.

> Mother,
>
> I will not attend the gathering. I have not agreed to any match, and I will not be presented as if I have. Do not make promises in my name again.
>
> I am safe. I am not coming home for Christmas. If you want to speak to me, write to me as your daughter, not as an heiress or a piece of the family’s future.
>
> Mara

She read it twice. The words looked too plain for what they meant. No elegant curse, no grand declaration. Only a boundary, written in her own hand.

“Does it say what you want?” James asked.

“It says what I can say tonight.”

“Then it’s enough.”

She folded the parchment, sealed it with plain wax, and held it out to the owl waiting on the kitchen windowsill. It took the letter and vanished into the dark.

Mara watched until the night swallowed its wings. She was not suddenly fearless. She could already hear her mother’s reply in her head: cold, precise, disappointed. The old house still had a place in her memory, and memory did not obey a train ticket. But the words were gone from her hand. They belonged to her now, not to the people who had written the first letter.

James reached across the table, palm open. This time she took it without hesitation.

Inside, the fire was bright, and the pudding was warm, and no one asked her to wear a crest or smile at a stranger. Her mother’s letters would come. The Black portraits would keep talking. The future would not become easy because she had chosen a different house for Christmas.

But the choice was hers.

Mara stepped into the sitting room with James beside her and Sirius waiting by the fire. For the first time, the name she carried felt less like a command and more like a story she could decide how to tell.

And for now, she was home.
