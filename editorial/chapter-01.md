# Chapter One

*Southampton, 10 April 1912*

By half past eleven Arwen had tipped three men for carrying her trunk and could no longer say where it was. The third man, who wore a white jacket and had not carried it at all, assured her that everything marked *Wanted on Voyage* would come to her cabin. She would have liked to ask where they put the things one wanted only after it was too late to go back for them. Instead she thanked him. He had six cabin numbers written on his cuff and no room for another.

The black dispatch case had remained in her hand from the moment she left the train. It contained two contracts, her sister’s address in Philadelphia, and a ham sandwich wrapped in a page of the *Liverpool Echo*. At the station that morning her brother-in-law had put in the first contract while she was buying the sandwich. She had watched him do it through the glass of the refreshment-room door. He had pulled his glove off with his teeth to get at the catch, a little intimacy with her property that she had found harder to bear than any of the things he had said at the funeral.

‘One signature,’ Ivor had told her. ‘Then it needn’t spoil your crossing.’

There were in fact two places to sign. He had marked both with little crosses in pencil, as William used to mark proofs when the correction was too obvious to bother putting into words.

In her cabin there was a vase without flowers and an armchair large enough to sit in without touching either arm. The bed had been turned down an inch or two, though it was hours yet to bedtime. At home she had been sleeping on William’s side because she had grown tired of waking in the night and putting a hand out. Here she could sleep in the middle. The thought came to her with such pleasure that she took off her hat and sat quite still until it had passed.

Then she ate half the sandwich over the washbasin. A strip of newsprint had stuck to the butter. She scraped it off with a fingernail and read the three words she had salvaged: *the men insist*.

Well, they did.

She put the rest away and went up to see England go.

There were men on the quay with their hands in their pockets who had plainly come to see a ship and not to part from anyone. Arwen envied them. A woman beside her had brought a little boy to the rail and was trying to make him wave to somebody he couldn’t distinguish in the crowd. At last he waved to a horse. The horse went on drinking from a bucket.

When the quay began to slide past, the boy asked if the horse would be there when they came back. His mother said yes so quickly that Arwen turned to look at her. She was quite young. She held the boy with his back to her and looked over his head while he waved.

For a few minutes everybody on deck seemed to have somebody to whom they could still make themselves understood at a distance. Arwen had seen Ivor off at the station, not the dock. He disliked the smell of a tideway. She had reached her compartment and found a bunch of violets on the seat, bought and paid for by him, and had been obliged to lower the window so she could call her thanks. He had done a great many kind things since William died. It was the reckoning up of them, whenever they differed, that made her wish he would do fewer.

Some way along the docks a sound like gunfire broke over the water. The boy ducked. A second report followed, then another, and across the narrowing stretch of water an empty liner began to move where no liner ought to be moving. Its stern came away from the quay. Ends of broken rope lay in the water; one was still running in a little wake of its own. Arwen could see a man on the other ship’s deck standing with his feet wide apart and holding his cap down with both hands, as though the cap were the one thing he had been told not to lose.

A passenger said it was the *New York*. Another said, with the indignation of a man whose cab had taken the wrong turning, that they were going to collide.

‘Here, sir. Give the lady room to get the child down.’

The voice came from behind Arwen. She reached up without looking and took the boy under the arms while his mother freed the hem of his coat from a fitting at the rail. He was heavier than she expected, and warm clear through his wool. Once his feet touched the deck, Arwen steadied him with a hand at the nape of his neck. Then his mother crouched and put both arms round him. ‘Stand on my foot if you want to see,’ she told him. ‘Here. On my good shoe.’

Arwen turned then. She saw the shoulder of a dark coat and the side of a face beneath a peaked cap. The man had already moved on. Ten years could change a man’s shoulders; they could put lines beside his mouth that had not been there before. They could do remarkably little about his voice.

She watched him go as far as a gate she couldn’t follow him through. At the gate he turned his head to answer someone, and she saw, quite uselessly, that he still had the small white notch in his left eyebrow.

It was Harold Lowe.

A tug went across the gap trailing a wash the colour of dirty linen. The crowd pressed back to the rail, and the young mother stood on tiptoe to see over it. Arwen gave up her place. She was suddenly afraid somebody would ask her what she thought had happened, and she would tell them the wrong thing.

---

The trunk came while she was reading the second contract. Mrs Hesketh had drawn it up herself on the back of a spoiled order for three thousand funeral cards. She had been forewoman at the bindery since before William took Arwen there and showed her how much gold leaf a woman could lift on the point of a brush without appearing to lift anything at all. Under Mrs Hesketh’s terms, the women would buy the bindery from Arwen in quarterly payments. The first payment was due in July. The sum would not have kept Arwen at a New York hotel for a week.

Under the other contract a firm in Manchester would buy the whole works outright. They wanted the two machines downstairs and the presses upstairs. They hadn’t asked to see the women.

‘Where would you like it, madam?’ the steward said.

She told him at the foot of the bed. It wasn’t until he lowered it that she saw the gilt initials on the lid. W.V. The trunk had been William’s. They had bought it for a trip to Brussels they kept putting off, first for the work and then for his health. She had once reproached him for buying a thing ten years before he needed it. He had said, ‘I was under the impression we’d have the ten years.’ It had been an ordinary quarrel, and he had said it to win. Some days she missed him most when she remembered how cheaply he could win.

‘There is writing paper in the drawer, madam,’ the steward said. He had seen the papers in her lap. ‘If you have anything for the shore, you can leave it with the purser’s office. There’ll be another opportunity at Queenstown tomorrow.’

She thanked him. When he left, she opened the desk drawer. The writing paper was thick and white and bore the name of the ship. She wrote *Dear Ivor*, then laid the pen down.

Her sister had offered her a room until she found her feet. A room meant something to Catrin, who had six children and took in sewing. In her last letter she had enclosed a drawing of the upstairs landing with an arrow pointing to a door. *It bolts on the inside,* she had written. Arwen had laughed at that, and then had folded the drawing along the little wall and put it in her purse. The money from Manchester would let her arrive as a visitor. Without it she might still be there at Christmas, hearing Catrin tell one of the children not to ask when Aunt Arwen was going home.

She took a fresh sheet and addressed it to Mrs Hesketh. *I ought to tell you that the Manchester offer was not made after William died.* That much she got down. William had known about it for three months. He had discussed it with Ivor while she was downstairs balancing the books, and neither man had thought to ask her what she would do if there was no shop to go to on Monday. The last time William had been strong enough to go in, Mrs Hesketh had come down to help him with the stairs. At the bottom he had kissed her cheek. Afterwards she had told Arwen about it in a voice that made it plain she had considered the kiss a pension of sorts; something earned over twenty years and finally paid.

Arwen drew a line through what she’d written. It wasn’t the sort of thing one told a woman by post unless one had something better to tell her in the next sentence.

She tried to read the contracts again. In the margin beside *fixtures and fittings* she caught herself drawing the same eyebrow, with a break where it met the nose. She blotted it out, angry at the ease with which her hand had remembered. She had spent half a morning once watching that cut clot and open again because Harold wouldn’t stop talking. He’d got it carrying a rolled carpet down his father’s stairs. In the version he told his brothers it was a fight on the quay. She had been the only person allowed to laugh at him about it.

Her trunk stood shut at the foot of the bed. She thought about unpacking. Instead she took her coat from its hook and went back upstairs.

---

At the sheltered end of the promenade the deck chairs were still mostly unclaimed. Arwen sat in one until a steward told her it belonged to somebody, then stood with her back to the deckhouse and watched a stretch of coast she couldn’t name. The ship had been under way again for hours. She had seen every sort of cap except the one she was looking for.

She had just made up her mind to go inside when a man coming along the deck interrupted his own whistle.

‘Arwen Prys,’ he said.

He took his cap off. That made it worse somehow. A man in uniform could be looked at as part of the ship; a man with his cap in his hand expected you to answer him.

‘Vaughan,’ she said. The name came out faster than she meant it to.

‘Yes. I heard.’

‘Did you?’

‘Not about the black,’ he said. His eyes went from her coat to her face. ‘I’m sorry. I heard you’d married.’

‘In February,’ she said. ‘He died in February. We were married seven years.’

‘What was his name?’

It was the first time anyone had asked. The people who knew William did not need to, and the people who did not know him said *your husband*, which made him sound like a position she had been dismissed from.

‘William,’ she said.

Harold nodded, once. He did not say he was sorry a second time. He set his cap on the rail, thought better of it and took it up again.

‘You’ve got your own ship at last,’ she said.

‘Somebody ought to tell the captain.’

She hadn’t meant it as a joke. His mouth went up at one side, and she was shown, before she had time to look away, exactly what she had once been willing to go without merely to have him smile at her across a room.

‘Fifth officer,’ he said. ‘On this one. How far are you going?’

‘Philadelphia, by way of New York. My sister’s there.’

‘Catrin with the cold hands?’

‘They’ve got six children now. She’s warm all the time.’

He laughed. The sound was so familiar that she looked out to sea as though somebody on land might have heard it too.

‘Did you know it was me this afternoon?’ she asked.

‘I knew when you set that boy down. You used to put a hand on the back of my neck just like that when you wanted me to duck under your mother’s washing.’

‘You used to walk into it on purpose.’

‘Once. I did it once on purpose and you’ve charged me for it ever since.’

The deck creaked under the feet of a couple walking past. Arwen waited until they were out of earshot. ‘Why didn’t you say anything?’

‘The *New York* was adrift.’

‘After that.’

He looked down at his cap. His thumb went round the inside of the band. ‘I thought if you wanted me to speak to you, you’d have stayed.’

It was reasonable, and she had known him to be reasonable in just this way before, to leave a gap so wide between what she’d done and what she’d wanted that only she could be blamed for not crossing it. In Liverpool she had learned to say what she meant to people she had no reason to be afraid of. It turned out not to travel.

‘I did want you to,’ she said.

He stopped turning the cap.

She could have let it stand, a modest little confession about an afternoon that they’d both lived through without much harm. Instead she said, ‘I want you to now.’

He looked at her then. She had been wearing gloves since the funeral, even at home sometimes when she sat up after supper and had forgotten to take them off. Her hands were bare now. She could feel the cold in the tender patch where her wedding ring had been turned too often. She closed her hand before she knew she meant to.

‘All right,’ he said. ‘I was glad to see you. I was also bloody angry, which took me rather by surprise. I didn’t think I had it left in me to be angry with you.’

‘What were you angry about?’

‘You were standing ten feet away. And for a second I thought, There she is. As if I’d been expecting you all this time. I felt a fool.’

There was no good way to answer that without telling him that she had put his letters in the grate after she got engaged and gone back to see in the morning whether any of the handwriting had lasted the night. She had found three words on a triangle of paper: *in fine weather*. She used to know the rest of the sentence, but she didn’t anymore.

‘I was glad, too,’ she said. It was the best she could do for him just then, and she saw from the way he drew breath that it was not as little as she’d thought.

From inside came the muffled sound of someone striking piano keys one at a time. Each note was given long enough to show what was wrong with it. Harold turned his cap over and put it on.

‘I have to go,’ he said.

‘Of course.’

‘Do you still get up before everybody else?’

‘There hasn’t been anybody else to get up before lately.’

She hadn’t meant to say it. He looked at her, not quickly and not away, and for a moment she thought he might reach for her hand. He did not. She was glad of that nearly as much as she was sorry.

‘I can usually get a cup of tea before seven,’ he said. ‘If you’re out here and I’m free, I’ll bring you one.’

‘I take it without sugar now.’

‘You never took it with sugar. You put yours in mine and pretended you hadn’t.’

She had forgotten that. There had been a summer when there wasn’t much in the house and she had thought she was doing it so cleverly. She stood looking at him after he said goodbye and had the peculiar sensation of having been paid back a kindness she’d never put down in any book.

Down in her cabin the writing paper had lifted at the corners. The half sandwich was still in the dispatch case. She finished it standing up, then sat at the desk and read Mrs Hesketh’s terms again. There was a genuine difficulty with July. It was the quietest month in the bindery, and Mrs Hesketh, like everybody else who asks for something she thinks she won’t get, had tried to make the first concession before the other person could ask for it. Arwen put her finger on the date and did the sums once more. In September the school orders would come in.

She fetched the pen. *Dear Mrs Hesketh. I can wait until September for the first payment. Before you agree to anything, there is one expense neither of us has allowed for.* The shaft that drove the folding machine ran off the printer’s motor downstairs. It would cost money to run the bindery without it, and whoever bought the presses had no reason to leave it in place. She drew the lamp nearer and set it out for her, as frankly as she would have done across the worktable if William had not been there to tell them they needn’t trouble their heads. Then she wrote to Ivor. It took her less than a page, and when she had finished it she did not read it over.

At the purser’s office a clerk told her the letters would go ashore in the morning. He reached for the case as well as the envelopes; she must have looked as though she meant to give him both.

‘Only those,’ she said, and shifted the case to her other hand. There was still a contract in it with two little crosses on the last page. She would keep it. It would be useful, next time a man told her where to sign, to know what a blank line looked like when she had left it blank herself.

On the way back to her cabin she passed a clock and stopped to set her watch by it. Then she wound the watch, though she had wound it that morning. She didn’t know whether Harold would be free. She knew she would be awake.
