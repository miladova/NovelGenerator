THE NAME SHE KEPT
Warsaw, 1943

I

The first thing Marianna recognized was not his face but the way he took off a glove.

He caught the tip of the middle finger between his teeth, pulled, and frowned at the glove as though it had disappointed him. Twenty years ago he had done that with a pair of woolen mittens outside her father’s house. She remembered being irritated by it. She had loved him enough to believe she could teach him better manners.

Now there was a black car behind him and an eagle above the breast pocket of his field-gray tunic.

Her daughter was saying, “Mama, your parcel.”

The parcel had slipped against Marianna’s hip. She gripped it, crushing the paper around two turnips and a heel of bread. Across the pavement the officer turned toward a corporal who was holding open the car door. A tram shrieked on the curve. For a moment its iron complaint seemed to come from inside her own head.

“Mama?”

“Keep walking, Zosia.”

She said it too late.

The officer looked directly at her.

She had imagined him dead so often that she had acquired a private collection of his deaths: fever in a foreign hospital, a train accident, a grave beneath an unfamiliar spelling of his name. Never this. Never a leather belt, polished boots, and two soldiers making room for him on a Warsaw pavement.

“Marianna?”

His voice was older. Nothing else could have made her stop.

People divided around them without appearing to notice. In occupied Warsaw, a Polish woman addressed by a German officer was a spectacle from which sensible people averted their eyes.

“Pani Frontczak,” she said.

He glanced at Zosia. Her daughter had her father’s straight dark brows and Marianna’s mouth, currently set in the expression she wore when a teacher asked a foolish question. Fourteen, and already unable to conceal contempt properly.

“Your daughter?” he asked in Polish.

“Yes.”

“I didn’t know.”

“You would not.”

The corporal shifted beside the car. The officer dismissed him with a movement of his bare hand. On the fourth finger was a pale band where a ring had been.

“I thought you had left Warsaw.”

“I thought many things too.”

She heard the danger in her own voice and lowered her eyes to the parcel. He was a major. She could read enough German insignia now to know the distinction between a man who might kill someone on impulse and a man who could arrange for it to happen elsewhere.

“May we go, Herr Major?”

He flinched at the title. She was glad. Then she hated herself for wasting gladness on anything so small.

“Of course.”

His gaze remained on her face. She had been twenty-two when he last saw her, and she was forty-two now. There were gray hairs at her temples, a missing back tooth, a burn on her wrist from a kitchen in which she no longer lived. Let him account for those things if he could.

She took Zosia by the elbow.

Behind them he said, softly, “You still turn your collar under when it rains.”

She did not look back.

They walked two streets before Zosia spoke.

“How does he know you?”

Marianna watched a woman scraping something from the sole of her shoe with a stick. A normal, foolish task. She wanted to stand beside her and discuss the difficulty of keeping shoes decent.

“From before.”

“Before what?”

“Before you were born.”

“That doesn’t answer anything.”

“No,” Marianna said. “It doesn’t.”

At the next corner she made them cross, although it added ten minutes to their journey. She looked in a shop window to see whether anyone followed. In the glass she saw a woman clutching food and a girl angrily keeping pace with her. She could not find the person who had once waited at a station for a man named Friedrich Adler.

That person had been left there in 1923.

II

At home, Zosia set the turnips in a basin and examined the damaged bread as though it might provide evidence.

Their kitchen had formerly been a pantry. The larger rooms of the apartment belonged to a widow and her two sons, who repaired shoes, quarreled, and observed the distinction between stolen leather and leather which had merely become available. Marianna and Zosia slept on a narrow bed behind a curtain. Above it hung a photograph of Jan Frontczak in a suit borrowed for their wedding.

Marianna took off her hat. Her hands would not obey her at the pin.

Zosia came over and removed it without a word. That nearly undid her.

“He was a friend,” Marianna said.

“A German friend.”

“Yes.”

“You speak German because of him?”

“Partly.”

She had told Jan everything before they married. Or nearly everything. She had omitted the indignity of the station: the little case, the new gloves, the hours she had spent pretending to wait for a delayed train instead of a man who had not come. Jan had known about Friedrich. Zosia had not.

“There were Germans before there were these Germans,” she said, and immediately heard how feeble it sounded.

“Was he different?”

“Yes.”

“People don’t turn into something else because they put on clothes.”

Marianna went to the stove. Under the lid, yesterday’s soup had thickened into a gray paste.

“No,” she said. “They don’t.”

That evening Zosia went downstairs for water. She returned carrying the bucket and a small envelope.

“The porter gave me this.”

Marianna knew the handwriting before she touched it.

Inside was a sheet folded once.

I owe you an explanation. Tomorrow, eleven, at the cemetery. Near your father’s grave. I shall come alone. F.

No address. No official stamp. He had found their building within hours.

She held the paper over the stove, then drew it back. Burning it was an answer of a kind. Keeping it was worse. She tore it into little pieces and worked them into the damp fuel until the handwriting had become black flakes.

“Are you going?” Zosia asked.

“No.”

But in the morning Marianna washed her face twice.

Her father’s grave was at Powązki. He had died in 1921, still calling the city’s independence a miracle, though he complained of the price of every candle lit to celebrate it. Friedrich had helped carry flowers to the funeral. There had been rain, his wet wool coat, her hand briefly hidden in his pocket with his.

She went because he knew where she lived. She told herself this on the tram and again at the cemetery gates. She needed to discover what he wanted. A woman could not afford to leave an officer curious.

He wore civilian clothes beneath a dark overcoat. They did not disguise his bearing. His hair, formerly the color of chestnuts, had gone almost entirely gray at the sides.

“You came,” he said.

“Do not mistake it for pleasure.”

“I won’t.”

There were fresh flowers on the grave. She looked at them, then at him.

“You have no right.”

“I remembered he liked—”

“You have no right to remember my father.”

He bent and gathered up the flowers. It was awkward, almost comic, seeing him stand with the cheap little bunch in his hand.

For several moments neither spoke.

“When did you arrive?” she asked.

“February.”

“And you have been looking for old acquaintances?”

“No. Yesterday was an accident.”

“What do you do?”

“Transport allocations. Army supplies. I’m attached to an administrative staff.”

“You sound like a man describing a position at a bank.”

He looked down at the flowers.

She remembered his hands under a bicycle chain, black with grease. In 1922 he had come east for a German machinery firm, selling replacement parts to workshops which could rarely afford them. He had been twenty-six, a veteran already, with a limp that appeared in cold weather and a Polish mother whose language he spoke more carefully than she did.

He had rented a room from her aunt. Marianna, earning money typing invoices, had corrected the Polish in his sales letters. The first time he kissed her, she had laughed because he asked permission after he had already done it.

“Why didn’t you come to the station?” she asked.

She had intended to ask about his uniform, his work, the ease with which he had located her. Instead, the twenty-two-year-old stepped forward and humiliated them both.

“My brother came for me the night before. My father had suffered a stroke. We crossed the border that morning.”

“You could have sent a telegram.”

“Yes.”

“You could have written.”

“I did write. Later. I said my father needed me and that you should come to Breslau.”

“I received it.”

She remembered every line. He had written of duty and family and a room in his parents’ house. Nothing of the life they had agreed upon in Warsaw, the small civil ceremony, the flat with the crooked floor. She was to come to him. It had already been decided.

“You never answered,” he said.

“I answered by not coming.”

“I was angry.”

“So was I.”

“I thought you would relent.”

“Of course you did.”

He put the flowers on the neighboring grave, where a stone angel had lost its head.

“Was your marriage happy?” he asked.

The question was so nakedly selfish that she almost walked away.

“Yes.”

“Was?”

“Jan was taken in a roundup in 1940. We received one letter. Then an official notice.”

“I’m sorry.”

“He was a tram mechanic. He could make a broken clock work with a piece of wire. He sang terribly and believed himself good at it.” She stopped. These were not things for Friedrich to possess. “Do not ask me about him again.”

Friedrich nodded.

She looked toward the path. A man was kneeling by a grave, brushing dead leaves away with his hat.

“Are you married?” she asked.

“My wife died in 1938. No children.”

She had nothing to say to this. His grief could not be fitted anywhere without displacing her own.

“If you need anything,” he said, “food, medicine—”

“No.”

“Please. There must be something I can—”

“You have already found my daughter. Stay away from her.”

“I would never hurt your child.”

She looked at him until he lowered his eyes.

Then she left him beside her father’s grave, with nothing in his hands.

III

Three days later the ghetto began to burn.

At first the smoke lay low enough to be mistaken for weather. Then it rose beyond the roofs, and people standing in bread queues tilted their heads to follow it. Gunfire carried strangely between buildings. Sometimes it sounded close enough to be in the next courtyard; sometimes the city swallowed it entirely.

At the sewing room where Marianna worked, a sleeve lay beneath her needle while she listened to an explosion. The machine had been manufactured in Germany. Its small metal plate had annoyed her for months. Now she rubbed a thumb over the letters until the forewoman told her she was staining a customer’s cloth.

That night Zosia came home late.

“Water,” she said before Marianna asked. “There was a queue.”

Her bucket was dry inside.

Marianna shut the door.

“Where were you?”

Zosia looked at the bucket. Her face altered, not into guilt but into calculation.

“Don’t lie again,” Marianna said.

For a moment she thought her daughter would defy her. Then Zosia sat on the bed.

“There’s a boy.”

“A boy.”

“Not like that.”

The explanation came in pieces. The boy was named Dawid. He was eleven, perhaps twelve; he had become uncertain of his own age. His mother had once brought mending to the woman who kept the sewing room’s accounts. In March he had been brought out of the ghetto and hidden in a storage loft behind a cobbler’s shop. Then the cobbler’s brother had returned unexpectedly. Now Dawid was in a cellar two courtyards away, and Zosia had been taking him food.

“Who else knows?”

“Mrs. Wilk. And Antek.”

“Antek is fifteen.”

“He can still know things.”

“God preserve me from children who know things.”

Zosia stood. “He couldn’t stay there without food.”

“No. He couldn’t.”

That answer stopped her.

Marianna sat down because her legs had begun to shake. She had been afraid, every day, of losing her daughter to the ordinary machinery of occupation: an unlucky tram journey, a raid, a soldier’s bad temper. Now there was a specific cellar, a specific child, a danger Zosia had chosen without consulting her.

“You have put everyone here at risk.”

“I know.”

“You do not know. Knowing is not the same as being able to say it.”

Zosia’s eyes filled, but she held her mother’s gaze.

“What should I have done?”

Marianna had no answer she could bear to hear aloud.

She went that night.

Dawid sat on an upturned crate in a coal cellar, with his feet off the floor because of rats. Someone had given him a cap much too large for him. When Marianna opened the door, he stood so quickly that his head struck a shelf.

“I’m Zosia’s mother.”

He looked beyond her.

“She’s at home.”

“Did I do something?”

The question entered Marianna like a needle.

“No. Sit down.”

She handed him a jar of soup. He took it politely and waited. Only when she turned her back to examine the stairs did he begin to eat.

There was a book beside the crate, a damaged atlas. She asked whether he liked geography.

“It’s not mine,” he said, as though that mattered more.

“May I look?”

He passed it to her. Several pages had been torn out. On the map of Europe someone had drawn a pencil line around the sea.

“That was my sister,” he said. “She wanted there to be a road.”

Marianna returned the book.

“Is there anyone we can send word to?”

“Mrs. Wilk says not to ask.”

“Then I won’t.”

He tightened his hands around the jar. Beneath the cap his ears stuck out, delicate and dirty. She thought of Zosia at eleven, indignant over a haircut.

On the way home she stopped beneath an arch and retched, quietly, bringing up nothing. Afterward she wiped her mouth and went upstairs to discuss food.

Mrs. Wilk, who kept the accounts, knew a woman outside the city. There might be room for a child there. There might also be papers, if enough money could be found. Every sentence contained a might, and each might had a price.

Marianna sold her wedding earrings. They were small garnets, worth less than she had hoped. She retained the wedding ring because it was plain gold and because she was not yet prepared to discover what a stranger would give for it.

Friedrich sent no more notes.

For a week she almost succeeded in not thinking of him.

IV

The cellar was discovered on a Thursday, though not by the Germans.

A man who collected scrap entered the courtyard and saw Zosia coming up the stairs with an empty dish. Later he returned. He spoke to the caretaker. By evening he knew enough to ask for money.

His name was Borkowski. Marianna had seen him once at a market, weighing a woman’s copper pan on a balance which favored him. He came to the apartment while the widow’s sons were out.

“A dangerous business,” he said. “Young people don’t understand.”

Marianna stood in the doorway so that he could not see Zosia behind her.

“What do you want?”

He named a sum larger than everything she owned.

“For silence?”

“For assistance. I’m trying to keep you from trouble.”

“And after we pay?”

“Then we shall see.”

There it was. No promise even worth breaking.

He looked over her shoulder. “Pretty girl. You should take care.”

Marianna shut the door in his face. The latch sounded impossibly frail.

They moved Dawid before dawn into the room behind the sewing shop. Mrs. Wilk had a contact who could take him out with a family traveling under false names, but not until Monday. Four nights. Borkowski returned to the cellar and found it empty. At noon he appeared outside the shop.

Marianna watched him from behind the curtain. He smiled when he saw it move.

She had Friedrich’s note by memory, though she had burned it. He had given her no address. Yet an officer attached to army transport was not difficult to find if one was willing to ask the wrong questions in the wrong language.

She waited outside an army office with a basket of mending. At the entrance she asked for Major Adler. The sentry told her to leave. She said she had repaired a coat and had been instructed to deliver it personally.

There was no coat in the basket.

She stood while the sentry telephoned. Sweat ran down her back beneath her blouse. She thought of all the disasters that began with someone asking a simple second question.

Then Friedrich was there.

He did not smile. That, at least, he understood.

He took her to an unoccupied office with a map on the wall. Colored pins marked places where other people lived.

“What is it?”

“A man is threatening my daughter.”

Friedrich closed the door.

“His name?”

She told him. She had intended to say only that Borkowski was extorting them, but Friedrich asked why, and she could not invent quickly enough.

“There is a child,” she said.

He became very still.

“A Jewish child?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“No.”

“Marianna—”

“You will not have his address.”

Footsteps passed in the corridor. Friedrich waited until they had gone.

“Do you understand what you are asking?”

“I understand what happens if I ask no one.”

He went to the window. Below, an engine started and failed, started and caught.

“I can’t simply have a Polish civilian arrested without a reason.”

She stared at him.

He turned away from her expression.

“I mean I cannot do it without attention.”

“Tell him to leave us alone. Frighten him. Men in your uniform seem to manage that easily.”

“If he has already spoken to the police—”

“Then you can go back to your allocations.”

He put both palms on the desk.

“I am trying to think.”

“So am I. All day. All night. It has made very little difference.”

The anger deserted her so abruptly that she had to sit. He reached for the water carafe. She shook her head.

“I used to imagine seeing you again,” she said. “Even after I married. I would have been wearing a beautiful dress. You would have understood immediately what a fool you had been.”

He made a small sound, almost a laugh.

“Don’t,” she said. “It wasn’t funny to me.”

“No.”

“I did not imagine begging you to keep a man from selling my daughter.”

He sat opposite her. His face was tired in the ordinary way of middle-aged faces. She resented that ordinariness most of all.

“Give me until tomorrow.”

“I have until tomorrow. He said he would return at six.”

Friedrich wrote Borkowski’s name on a sheet, then his description, the courtyard, the market where he traded. His handwriting was still slanted steeply to the right.

“Where will you be?” he asked.

“At work.”

“Go home afterward. Do not try to find me here again.”

It was the first order he had given her. She carried it out because she had nothing better.

V

The next afternoon Borkowski was waiting across from the sewing shop.

Marianna saw his hat first, then the fleshy roll above his collar. He stepped into her path as she came out.

“You’ve been clever,” he said.

She could not tell whether he was frightened or pleased.

“I don’t know what you mean.”

“Oh, you know.” He moved nearer. “A German officer. A woman your age.”

His breath smelled of onions. She wanted to strike his mouth with the basket.

“He told me you’re doing work for the army. That I’m interfering with deliveries.” Borkowski smiled, showing a brown tooth. “Should I believe him?”

“You should leave.”

“Perhaps I shall ask someone who outranks him.”

A tram passed behind him. When its noise receded, another voice said, in German, “This man?”

Friedrich stood a few paces away. Beside him was a military policeman with a metal gorget resting against his chest.

Borkowski took off his hat.

Friedrich spoke in quick German. Marianna caught obstruction, repeated warning, investigation. The policeman took Borkowski’s arm. Borkowski began explaining, first in Polish, then in scraps of German. He looked at Marianna with astonishment, as though she had violated an agreement.

They put him in a car.

Friedrich did not approach her until it had driven off.

“He will be held,” he said.

“For how long?”

“Long enough for you to move the child.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know yet.”

She gripped his sleeve before she could stop herself. The cloth was finer than anything she had touched in years.

“What have you told them?”

“That he is extorting money from civilians employed on army work.”

“Is that all?”

“Yes.”

She let go.

“Thank you.”

He looked almost relieved. She despised him for that, then felt the weariness of having to despise a man while needing him.

“There may be a way to get you both out of Warsaw,” he said.

“Both?”

“You and your daughter.”

“And Dawid?”

He hesitated.

“No,” she said.

“I have a contact who could arrange work farther west. Documents. Somewhere safer.”

“Somewhere under occupation.”

“Safer than here.”

She looked at the buildings above the street. A blanket hung from a window, airing in the smoke-laden light. Someone had sewn three different fabrics together to make it large enough for a bed.

“You still do it,” she said.

“What?”

“Find a place for me and expect me to follow.”

“This is not Breslau in 1923.”

“No. It is Warsaw in 1943. You have become much harder to refuse.”

He looked away.

“I didn’t mean it that way.”

“I believe you.”

She left him there. Her gratitude accompanied her like something she had stolen and could not return.

That night she told Zosia what she had done.

Her daughter listened without interruption, sitting beside the stove with a potato in one hand and a knife in the other. At the end she put both down.

“Was he your lover?”

Marianna had expected accusation. The plainness of the question surprised her.

“Yes.”

“Before Papa.”

“Long before.”

“Did Papa know?”

“Yes.”

Zosia picked up the knife, then laid it down again.

“Do you love him now?”

“No.”

This, at least, she could say immediately.

“But you did.”

“Yes.”

Outside, a door slammed. They both stopped talking until the footsteps on the stairs had passed.

“I don’t know how he can be the same person,” Zosia said.

Marianna sat beside her.

“I don’t know how to make him two.”

VI

The woman outside Warsaw could take Dawid, but the family with whom he was to travel had disappeared.

Mrs. Wilk said this in the room behind the shop while Dawid slept on a folded coat. Her voice was almost flat. Marianna understood that she had already spent her fear elsewhere.

“There is another route. A cart on Tuesday morning. He would need papers to reach the meeting place.”

“Can you get them?”

“Yes. Not by Tuesday.”

Through the wall came the measured clatter of a sewing machine. Someone was finishing a summer dress.

“I might be able to arrange transport,” Marianna said.

Mrs. Wilk looked at her for a long time.

“An army vehicle?”

“I said I might.”

“If you tell him anything, you are telling him about all of us.”

“I know.”

“Does he know my name?”

“No.”

The older woman pressed her fingertips to her closed eyes.

“I’m not brave enough to approve,” she said. “I’m not brave enough to stop you either.”

Marianna found Friedrich at the cemetery the next morning. She had sent a message through the army office, this time with an actual coat in her basket.

He listened to the plan without looking at her. Dawid needed to reach a road outside the city before dawn on Tuesday. No address of the refuge would be supplied. Zosia would accompany him because he trusted her. Marianna would go too.

“No,” Friedrich said at once.

“Then the child alone.”

“You misunderstand. I cannot put three civilians in a staff car and drive through a checkpoint without questions.”

“Then tell me what you can do.”

He rubbed his bad leg through the cloth of his trousers. She remembered massaging that knee in a rented room, the startling intimacy of being trusted with pain.

“A supply lorry leaves before six on Tuesday. I can inspect a depot on its route. You could be listed as laundry workers. The boy—”

He stopped.

“The boy can be my son,” she said.

“He doesn’t look like your daughter?”

“Neither do I. Is that a crime now too?”

“His accent?”

“He speaks Polish.”

“That is not what I asked.”

She felt suddenly sick. Friedrich knew the practical questions. He had lived here long enough to know exactly which details might condemn a child.

“He can be ill,” she said.

Friedrich nodded once.

“I must choose the driver.”

“Can you trust him?”

“I can make him afraid of losing his posting.”

There was no point asking for a cleaner instrument. She had come to him precisely because of what he could command.

He took a pencil from his pocket and wrote a time on the back of a cigarette packet. She memorized it.

“Do you remember the room on Hoża?” he asked.

“Do not do that.”

“I only meant—”

“I know what you meant. You cannot take me there because you have agreed to this.”

He put the packet away.

For the first time he looked old.

“What do you want me to say?” he asked.

She looked past him to the graves, the dates cut into stone, the years during which the dead had expected something quite different from what occurred.

“Nothing. I want you to come on Tuesday.”

VII

On Monday afternoon Dawid refused to leave without the atlas.

“It’s too large,” Marianna said.

“I can carry it.”

“You may need both hands.”

“I can put it under my shirt.”

“It will show.”

He held the book against his chest. She had seen the same stubbornness in Zosia over things incomparably less important and had called it bad behavior.

“How many pages would you need?” she asked.

“All of them.”

They settled on three. Dawid chose Poland, Palestine, and the page with the sea his sister had encircled. He folded them himself. The crack of the old paper was very loud.

Marianna took the rest of the atlas home. She told him she would keep it until he asked for it, then wondered whether making such promises to children was a form of theft.

That evening she washed him in a basin behind the shop. He stood stiffly in an oversized shirt while she scrubbed his neck. His ribs were visible, and there was a fading yellow bruise along one arm.

“Does that hurt?”

“Not anymore.”

She gave him Jan’s old scarf. It was too warm for May, but would help conceal the lower part of his face.

“Tomorrow, if anyone asks, your name is Piotr Frontczak.”

“I know.”

“Say it.”

“Piotr Frontczak.”

“And you have a fever.”

“Yes.”

“You must not call me pani.”

He studied her.

“What should I call you?”

She had thought through the route, the clothes, the money sewn into the hem of Zosia’s skirt. She had not prepared for this.

“Mama,” she said.

He nodded, accepting it as another task.

At home Zosia was writing a letter. She covered it with her hand when Marianna came in.

“To whom?”

“Antek.”

“What does it say?”

“That he owes me a book.”

Marianna waited.

“And that he shouldn’t do anything stupid if I don’t come back.”

She wanted to take the paper and burn it. Instead she sat on the edge of the bed.

“You don’t have to come.”

“Yes, I do.”

“No. You want to. That is different.”

Zosia folded the letter carefully.

“If you go and I stay, I’ll spend all morning imagining. I would rather be there.”

Marianna could not tell her that being there was no protection against imagining.

They lay down without undressing. Beyond the curtain the widow’s sons argued over a pair of boots. One of them began to whistle, stopped, began again. The normal sounds were harder to bear than silence.

After a while Zosia put her hand in her mother’s.

“Were you happy with him?”

“For a time.”

“Does that make it worse?”

Marianna watched the curtain move in a draft.

“Yes.”

Zosia’s fingers tightened. She did not ask anything more.

VIII

The lorry smelled of canvas, diesel, and damp wood.

Friedrich was waiting beside it in uniform. He checked his watch when they emerged from the passage with Dawid between them.

“You are late.”

“A patrol on the corner.”

He looked at Dawid. The boy lowered his eyes.

“Can he climb?”

“Yes,” Dawid said, before Marianna could answer.

The driver, a young soldier with pimples along his jaw, helped them into the rear. There were bundles of soiled linen and two wooden crates. Friedrich handed Marianna a folded document.

“Do not offer it unless you are asked.”

“And if I am?”

“Let me answer first.”

He climbed into the cab. The lorry moved.

Zosia sat with one arm around Dawid, who had begun shivering. Marianna tucked his hands between hers. His palms were slick. Through a gap in the canvas she watched a succession of doorways pass, a woman carrying milk cans, a man pulling down a shutter. She had never wanted so badly to be an unremarkable person on a pavement.

The first stop lasted less than a minute. Voices, a question, Friedrich’s reply. The engine strained and they went on.

At the second, a dog barked.

The sound changed everything. Dawid’s hands jerked in hers. Zosia bent toward him, and Marianna caught her wrist: do not whisper, do not soothe, do nothing anyone can notice.

Boots approached the rear of the lorry.

The canvas lifted.

A military policeman looked inside. He had pale lashes and a small shaving cut on his chin. His gaze moved from the linen to the women, then settled on Dawid.

“Papers.”

Marianna held out the document. He glanced at it.

“The boy.”

“My son. He is ill.”

Friedrich appeared behind him.

“They are under my authority.”

The policeman did not turn immediately. Marianna watched his eyes, the small calculation of how far his own importance extended.

“What illness?”

“Fever,” she said.

“Look at me.”

Dawid lifted his head.

The policeman reached toward the scarf.

Friedrich caught his wrist.

It was not violent. That made it worse. For one suspended moment the two Germans stood touching, with the child between them.

“I have signed for these workers,” Friedrich said. “If you wish to delay a military delivery, fetch your superior and we shall put it in writing.”

The policeman pulled his arm free.

“There are regulations.”

“Then bring the register.”

Friedrich took out his pen.

Marianna heard the dog panting somewhere below. The driver coughed. A drop of sweat traveled slowly between her shoulder blades.

At last the policeman stepped back.

“Proceed.”

Friedrich lowered the canvas himself. His hand shook once before it disappeared.

The lorry did not move.

They waited while someone shouted about another vehicle. Dawid began to draw breath in little noiseless gasps. Marianna pressed his face against her coat, pretending to adjust the scarf. She could feel his teeth knocking together.

Then the engine rose, the floor lurched beneath them, and the city began to loosen its hold.

They were put down beside a stand of trees where a narrow lane met the road. No depot was visible. The driver remained in the cab, studying something beyond the windscreen with elaborate attention.

Friedrich helped Zosia descend. She withdrew her hand as soon as her feet touched the ground, then looked ashamed of the gesture.

“Thank you,” she said in German.

He nodded as though she had given him a report.

Dawid climbed down by himself.

Marianna returned the document. Friedrich tore it into pieces and put them in his pocket.

“Follow the lane,” he said. “You should not be seen from the road.”

He had not asked who would meet them. She was grateful for that restraint in a way she could not express without giving too much.

“What will happen to you?” she asked.

“Probably nothing.”

“Probably.”

“There will be a record of my inspection. That is all.”

He looked at her, and she knew that he wanted something. Not a kiss. He had learned at least that much. He wanted her to tell him what this morning meant.

She could have said he had saved a life. It would have been true. He could have carried the words away and set them against everything he did not wish to examine.

“You must do it again,” she said.

His mouth moved slightly.

“For someone who does not know you,” she added.

Behind him the driver sounded the horn, a short embarrassed note.

Friedrich looked toward the lorry, then back at her.

“I don’t know whether I can.”

She thought this was the first completely honest thing he had said to her.

“Neither did I.”

She held out her hand. After a moment he took it. The old intimacy was absent. His palm was simply warm and human, and she wished that fact did not trouble her.

Then she turned and followed the children down the lane.

IX

The cart was late.

For forty minutes they waited behind a ruined outbuilding while the morning brightened. Dawid sat on a stone and watched the lane. Zosia counted something under her breath, lost her place, began again.

Marianna thought of the policeman returning with a register. She thought of Friedrich changing his mind. She thought of Borkowski walking out of a cell and beginning to ask questions. Fear could manufacture an entire population if given enough time.

At last a cart appeared, carrying sacks and a woman in a brown headscarf.

The woman did not stop until she had passed them. Then she climbed down, inspected a wheel, and said, without looking up, “It will rain before evening.”

“Not before the washing dries,” Marianna answered.

The woman straightened. She was younger than Marianna had expected, with reddened hands and a crooked front tooth.

“Which one?”

Dawid rose.

The woman looked him over, not unkindly but without ceremony.

“Can you help with hens?”

“I can learn.”

“That will do.”

She rearranged the sacks. Zosia helped Dawid climb onto the cart, then stepped back so quickly that Marianna knew she was trying not to cry.

Dawid touched the place under his shirt where the maps lay.

“You have the rest?” he asked Marianna.

“Yes.”

“Don’t let it get wet.”

“I won’t.”

He considered her, then held out his hand as though leaving a business appointment. She shook it because he had chosen that dignity.

The cart moved off. He did not look back until the bend. When he did, Zosia lifted one hand. Marianna could not make herself move.

They waited until the sound of the wheels had gone.

“Will he be all right?” Zosia asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Do you think he will?”

Marianna looked down the empty lane.

“I think he has a chance.”

They walked toward the station where Mrs. Wilk had said they could catch a train back. Marianna had rehearsed an explanation for their journey, then another explanation if the first failed. Her feet had begun to hurt. The ordinary discomfort steadied her.

After a while Zosia said, “You could have gone with him.”

“With Dawid?”

“With the major. When he offered.”

“So could you.”

“I wouldn’t.”

“Neither would I.”

Zosia considered this.

“Not even if he hadn’t been wearing it?”

Marianna knew what she meant.

“He was wearing it.”

They walked on.

X

In June, a girl Marianna had never seen brought a message to the sewing room. She asked for black thread, bought nothing, and left a folded scrap beneath a spool.

The hens are troublesome. The boy is less so.

No signature.

Marianna read it in the lavatory and sat for a long time on the closed lid, holding the paper against her mouth. Then she tore it up. This time she did not hesitate.

Borkowski did not return. Whether Friedrich had frightened him, transferred him, or arranged something worse, she never discovered. She asked once at the market and regretted asking. There were questions which left a trail without bringing an answer.

In July a letter arrived in the same steep handwriting.

I am being transferred. I will not come to see you. I have kept my promise about your daughter. I have tried to keep the other one, though I made no promise aloud.

There was no account of what he had done. She was glad of that. She did not want a list to judge or a debt renewed.

At the bottom he had written, I did love you.

She stood beside the stove with the letter in her hand. The sentence was both true and useless. She had loved him too. Neither fact had prevented a single thing.

Zosia came in carrying water and saw the paper.

“From him?”

“Yes. He’s leaving.”

“Are you going to answer?”

“No.”

Marianna opened the stove, then stopped. She took scissors from the drawer and cut away the lower part of the page. The rest she burned.

“What are you keeping?” Zosia asked.

“His name. The date.”

The girl waited.

“If someone asks one day how we took Dawid out, I want to remember exactly. That is all.”

She put the scrap inside Jan’s prayer book, not beside the photograph. There was no proper place for it. She would have to live with that too.

Later she brought out the atlas.

Its spine had split. Several loose pages had slipped behind the lining of her basket. She set them in order on the table while Zosia peeled potatoes. Outside, someone was beating a rug. Dust floated through the open window, briefly gold in the late light.

“Do you remember Papa taking us to the river?” Zosia asked.

“Which time?”

“When he dropped his hat in.”

“He said the wind took it.”

“He was bowing to you.”

“Yes,” Marianna said. “He was.”

They laughed, unexpectedly and badly, and for a moment she could see Jan’s affronted face as the hat turned on the water. Not the photograph, not the telegram, not the careful language of the notice. Jan himself, wet to the knees and pretending that the excursion was proceeding exactly as planned.

Zosia wiped her eyes with her wrist and complained about the potatoes.

Marianna threaded a needle. The atlas lay open to a world whose borders had already become obsolete. She drew the loose pages together, pierced the old cloth at the spine, and began to stitch.

The boy would need a book when he came for it.

She could do this much while she waited.
